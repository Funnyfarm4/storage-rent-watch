Arizona's self-storage law is **Title 33, Chapter 15 of the Arizona Revised Statutes, sections 33-1701 to 33-1706** [[az-ch]]. In this law the renter is the **occupant**, and the **operator** includes the owner, lessor or sublessor, an agent, or anyone authorized to manage the facility [[az-1701]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement matters too.

## The operator's lien

The operator has a **possessory lien** on everything stored in your unit, starting **from the date rent is unpaid and due**. It covers rent, late fees, labor or other charges, and expenses reasonably incurred in selling the property [[az-1703]]. "Default" means failing to do anything the rental agreement requires on time [[az-1701]].

Your rental agreement must tell you, in **at least 10-point bold type**, that the lien starts when rent is unpaid and due, that your property may be sold or disposed of if you're in default, that **you** must provide any insurance on your stored things, and that a late fee may be charged each month rent is late. It must also state the date rent is due and the date the late fee accrues [[az-1703]]. The agreement must ask you to disclose any lienholders on your property and whether you store any **protected property** (business records with customers' or patients' personal information, alcohol, firearms, or non-personal pharmaceuticals) [[az-1703]] [[az-1701]].

Every facility must keep this notice posted in its office or on the premises: *"Articles stored pursuant to a rental agreement may be sold or disposed of if any storage charges are overdue for more than thirty days."* [[az-1705]]

## Late fees

The operator may charge a reasonable late fee for each month you don't pay rent when due. The law says a reasonable late fee may be computed as **the greater of $10 a month or 20% of the monthly rent** [[az-1703]]. The operator must give you adequate notice before charging one: either the required bold statement in your rental agreement, or a notice by verified mail that a late fee may be charged in any month rent is late [[az-1703]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: Default and lockout

If you're in default, the operator **may deny you access** to your unit. Once you've been in default for **more than 30 days**, it may also move your property to a place of safekeeping until the sale [[az-1704]].

### Step 2: More than 30 days in default

The operator can foreclose its lien by selling your property at a **public sale, for cash**, only if you've been in default for **more than 30 days** [[az-1704]].

### Step 3: First notice of default

The operator must send a notice of default by **verified mail or email** to your last known address [[az-1704]]. Verified mail is any USPS mailing method that provides evidence of mailing. Your last known address is the postal or email address in your rental agreement, or in a later written change-of-address notice, so keep it current [[az-1701]].

### Step 4: Second notice (at least 14 days to pay)

At least **7 days after** the first notice was mailed, the operator must send a second notice by **first-class mail** to your last known address [[az-1704]]. It must include:

- a statement that the contents of your unit are subject to the operator's lien
- a statement of the operator's claim, showing the charges due on the date of the notice and any other charges that may accrue
- a demand for payment within a stated time **not less than 14 days after the mailing date** of the second notice, or **30 additional days** if your address is outside the continental United States
- a statement that unless the claim is paid in time, the contents will be sold (or protected property disposed of) at a **specified time and place**
- the **name, street address and phone number** of the operator or its agent you can contact

If there's a vehicle in the unit, the operator must also send matching notices to the vehicle's registered owner, including a description and VIN. At least **10 days before the sale**, it must notify by verified mail any lienholder it knows about [[az-1704]].

### Step 5: The sale

The sale is a public sale for cash, held at the time and place stated in the second notice [[az-1704]]. If **five or more bidders** unrelated to the operator attend, the sale and its proceeds are considered commercially reasonable [[az-1704]]. Protected property the operator knows about is **not sold**; it may be destroyed or surrendered to the appropriate authorities [[az-1704]].

A good-faith buyer takes the property free of anyone's rights, except protected property, which the buyer must return to the operator. If the operator follows the law, its liability to you from the sale is limited to the **net proceeds** [[az-1704]].

> **Vehicles, boats and trailers:** if you're in default for **more than 30 days**, the operator may hire a towing company to remove a vehicle, watercraft or trailer. At least **10 days before** the tow, it must send you notice by verified mail or email with the towing company's name, address and phone number. Once the tower has it, the operator isn't liable for it. A registered owner or lienholder may also pay the lien and take the vehicle back [[az-1704]].

## How to stop the sale

At **any time before the sale** (or before protected property is disposed of, whichever comes first), you may **pay the amount necessary to satisfy the lien** and redeem your property [[az-1704]]. That amount can include rent, late fees, labor or other charges, and sale expenses reasonably incurred [[az-1703]]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, and ask for a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. The law doesn't say a partial payment stops the sale, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the amount looks wrong, call the contact listed in your second notice [[az-1704]]. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

The operator must pay out the sale money in this order: reasonable costs of sale, other valid liens and security interests that rank ahead of it, its own lien, other recorded liens, and then **the balance to you, on demand** [[az-1704]].

If you don't claim your balance within **90 days after the sale**, the operator must pay it to the **Arizona Department of Revenue**. You then have **two years** from that payment to establish your right to the money; after that, unclaimed money goes into the permanent state school fund [[az-1704]]. Ask the operator in writing for your balance as soon as you can. If it's already been turned over, check the Department of Revenue's [unclaimed property page](https://azdor.gov/unclaimed-property), which says searching and filing a claim are free [[az-up]].

## Other Arizona rules

- **Value caps in the lease:** your rental agreement may limit the value of what you store, and that limit is treated as the maximum value of your stored property for all purposes [[az-1703]].
- **No living in units:** you may not use a storage unit as a residence, and the operator may not knowingly allow it [[az-1702]].
- **If the operator breaks the rules:** anyone harmed by a violation of the storage law may sue. On top of damages, the court may impose a civil penalty of up to **$500** and award reasonable attorney's fees and court costs [[az-1706]].
- **Warehouses:** the law doesn't apply to a warehouse operator unless it issues a warehouse receipt, bill of lading or other document of title for the stored property [[az-1701]].
- **Rent increases:** the law doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[az-ch]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
