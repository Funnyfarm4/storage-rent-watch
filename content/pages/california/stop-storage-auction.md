If your California storage unit is behind and you've gotten a notice of lien sale, you can still stop the sale. California's storage law, the Self-Service Storage Facility Act in Business and Professions Code sections 21700 to 21716 [[ca-ch]], gives you two main ways: pay the full lien amount in time, or formally dispute the lien with a declaration in opposition [[ca-05]]. Here's how each works.

## Step 1: Figure out which notice you have

California uses two notices. The first is a **preliminary lien notice**, which the owner may send once any rent or charges have gone unpaid for **14 consecutive days**. It must list what you owe and when each amount came due, and give a termination date **at least 14 days after mailing** [[ca-03]]. If you pay everything before that date, the process stops there [[ca-03]].

If you don't, the lien attaches on the termination date and the owner may lock you out, enter the space and move your things to safekeeping [[ca-05]]. It must then send a **notice of lien sale** with a sale date **at least 14 days from mailing**, plus a blank declaration in opposition [[ca-05]]. That sale date is your key deadline.

## Step 2: Check how the notices reached you

Both notices must go to your last known address and to the alternative contact you listed in your rental agreement, by certified mail, first-class mail with a certificate of mailing, or email [[ca-03]] [[ca-05]]. Your last known address is the mailing or email address in your latest rental agreement or in a later written change-of-address notice [[ca-01]].

Email is allowed only if your rental agreement says lien notices may be sent by email and you signed it consenting to that. If the owner can't show you actually received the email (for example, you replied, opened it, or viewed it in a password-protected app), it must resend the notice by mail [[ca-12]]. Also, the lien law doesn't apply at all unless your rental agreement asked for, and gave space for, an alternative person's name and address to receive notices [[ca-12]].

## Step 3: Check the amount

Compare the itemized statement with your rental agreement [[ca-03]]. The lien covers rent, labor, late fees and other charges under the agreement, plus expenses needed to preserve, sell or dispose of the property [[ca-02]]. Late fees are limited: none until rent is at least **10 days** late, only one per missed payment, the amount must be in your agreement, and it can't exceed **$10** (rent $60 or less), **$15** (rent over $60 but under $100), or the **greater of $20 or 15%** of monthly rent (rent $100 or more) [[ca-135]].

## Step 4, option A: Pay the full lien before the sale date

The notice of lien sale must say you can regain **full use of the space** by paying the full lien amount before the sale date [[ca-05]]. The lien keeps growing while rent goes unpaid [[ca-05]], so ask for a written payoff amount good through the day you'll pay. Pay in a way that leaves a record, get a receipt that says the lien is paid in full, and ask for written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

## Step 4, option B: Return the declaration in opposition

If you believe the lien isn't valid (for example, you already paid in full), you can oppose the sale with the declaration that came with the notice [[ca-05]].

- **Fill in every section**, including a brief reason the lien may not be valid and a **physical address and phone number** where you can be reached and served. If the owner can't contact or serve you there, the declaration is void [[ca-05]].
- **Sign it under penalty of perjury.** Only sign what's true [[ca-05]].
- **Send it by certified mail** so the owner **receives** it on or before the date in the notice. Keep the receipt [[ca-06]].
- **Keep your address current.** If you move before you're served with a lawsuit, give the owner your new address within 10 days or the declaration isn't valid [[ca-05]].

Once a valid declaration arrives in time, the owner can't simply sell. It can enforce the lien only by suing you in small claims court or another court, and can sell only if it gets a judgment in its favor [[ca-10]]. If it wins, you may owe court costs [[ca-05]]. California's courts have a free [small claims guide](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/small-claims) that explains the process [[ca-sc]].

If you withdraw the declaration in writing, or it's incomplete or unsigned, the owner may go ahead with the sale [[ca-06]].

## Step 4, option C: Pay the lien plus one month's rent and go to court

Before the sale, anyone claiming a right to the goods may pay the amount needed to satisfy the lien **plus one month's rent in advance**. The goods are then held, not sold, until a court order says what happens to them. If no court order is obtained within 30 days of the payment, the claimant must keep paying the monthly rent, or the owner may sell [[ca-09]]. This route is mainly for disputes about who owns the property; talk to free legal help before using it.

## Don't wait for auction day

After the notice deadline passes without payment or a valid declaration, the owner must advertise the sale, either once a week for two weeks in a local newspaper, or once in a newspaper plus once on a public auction website for seven days before the sale [[ca-07]]. A good-faith buyer takes the goods free of your rights **even if the owner didn't follow the law** [[ca-11]], so act before the sale.

## After a sale: claim the leftover money

After taking out the lien and the costs of sale, the owner must keep any excess on your behalf. You can claim it **at any time within one year of the sale**. After that, the owner pays what's left to the treasury of the county where the sale was held [[ca-07]]. Ask the facility in writing first and keep a copy; after a year, contact that county's treasurer.

## If the facility broke the rules

You can file a consumer complaint with the California Attorney General [[ca-ag]], and the free legal help above can tell you whether you have a claim.
