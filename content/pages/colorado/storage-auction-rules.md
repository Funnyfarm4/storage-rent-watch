Colorado's self-storage law is **C.R.S. Title 38, Article 21.5 (sections 38-21.5-101 to 38-21.5-105)** [[co-art]]. In this law the renter is the **occupant**, and the **owner** includes the owner, operator, lessor or sublessor, an agent, or anyone authorized to manage the facility or collect rent [[co-101]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement matters too.

## The owner's lien

The owner has a **lien on all personal property at the facility** for rent, labor or other charges, present or future, **including late fees**, plus expenses needed to preserve the property or reasonably incurred in selling or disposing of it. The lien starts when your things are brought in and lasts while the owner keeps possession, until the default is fixed or the property is sold or disposed of [[co-102]]. "Default" means failing to do anything the law or your rental agreement requires on time [[co-101]].

Before taking enforcement action, the owner must check whether a **financing statement** on the property has been filed with the Colorado Secretary of State, so a lender with an interest can be told too [[co-102]].

Your rental agreement must include a notice that everything stored will be **sold or otherwise disposed of if no payment is received for a continuous 30-day period**, and a provision asking you to disclose any lienholders with an interest in what you store [[co-1015]]. The owner must also keep a notice posted in a prominent place in its office saying that stored articles will be sold or disposed of if charges go unpaid for 30 days [[co-104]].

## Late fees

Your rental agreement may include a reasonable late fee for each month you don't pay rent in full when due. A late fee of **$20 or 20% of the monthly rent, whichever is greater**, for each late payment is treated as reasonable and not a penalty. The owner **can't collect a late fee as part of the lien unless the amount is stated in your rental agreement** or an addendum to it [[co-1015]].

## How notices are sent

The enforcement notice must be delivered **in person, by verified mail or by email** to your last-known address [[co-103]]. Verified mail is any U.S. Postal Service mailing method that provides evidence of mailing. Your last-known address is the postal or email address you gave in your latest rental agreement or in a later written change-of-address notice [[co-101]], so keep both current.

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: 30 days of continuous default

The owner can't take **any enforcement action** until you've been in default **continuously for 30 days** [[co-103]].

### Step 2: Written notice (at least 15 days to pay)

After 30 days of continuous default, the owner may begin enforcement only after notifying you **in writing**. The notice also goes to any lienholder the owner knows about through your rental agreement disclosure, a financing statement filed with the Secretary of State, or a written notice from the lienholder [[co-103]]. The notice must include:

- an **itemized statement** of the owner's claim, showing the amount due when the notice is sent and the date it became due
- a brief, general **description of the property** subject to the lien, good enough to identify it (a locked or sealed trunk, box or similar container may be described as such without listing what's inside)
- notice of any **denial of access** your rental agreement permits, with the **name, street address and phone number** of the owner or agent you can contact
- a **demand for payment** within a stated time **not less than 15 days after delivery** of the notice
- a **conspicuous statement** that unless the claim is paid in time, the property will be advertised and sold or otherwise disposed of at a **specified time and place**

If the owner emails a notice of a pending sale and gets **no response, return receipt or delivery confirmation** from that email address, it must send the sale notice by **verified mail** to your last-known postal address before going ahead [[co-103]].

### Step 3: Advertising the sale

After your payment deadline passes, the owner must advertise the sale either by publishing an ad **once a week for 2 consecutive weeks** in a periodical that circulates at least weekly in the county where the facility is, or in **any other commercially reasonable manner**. Advertising is considered commercially reasonable if at least **3 independent bidders** (people not related to, or sharing a financial interest with, the owner or another bidder) attend the sale at the advertised time and place [[co-103]].

### Step 4: The sale

The sale or other disposition must be held **on an online auction website that customarily conducts public auctions**, at the **storage facility**, or at the **nearest suitable place** to where the property is stored [[co-103]]. A good-faith buyer takes the property free of your rights even if the owner didn't follow the rules [[co-103]]. That doesn't erase your rights against the owner if the law's requirements weren't met, the sale didn't match the notice, or there was a willful violation [[co-103]].

> **Vehicles and boats:** if the lien is on a vehicle or watercraft and rent and other charges stay unpaid for **60 days**, the owner may have it towed by an **independent towing carrier** with current operating authority from the Colorado Public Utilities Commission. Once the tower takes possession, the owner isn't liable for it or for damage to it [[co-103]].

The owner isn't liable for identity theft or other harm from misuse of information in documents or electronic storage media that were part of your sold property, if it didn't actually know about them [[co-103]]. If you can, get papers and devices with personal information out early.

## How to stop the sale

**Before the sale or other disposition**, you may pay **the amount necessary to satisfy the lien plus the reasonable expenses** the owner incurred under the sale procedure, and redeem your property. Once the owner receives that payment, it **must return your property** [[co-103]]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, and ask for a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. The law doesn't say a partial payment stops the sale, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the amount looks wrong, call the contact listed in your notice [[co-103]]. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

The owner may pay its lien from the sale money, subject to the rights of any prior lienholder. If the sale was made in good faith and in a reasonable way, the owner must **hold any balance for you** (or a lienholder or other person with an interest). If nobody claims the balance **within 3 years of the sale date**, it becomes the **owner's property** with no further recourse [[co-103]]. So don't wait: ask the owner in writing for your balance as soon as you can, and keep a copy of your request.

## Other Colorado rules

- **Value caps in the lease:** if your rental agreement limits the total value of what you store, that limit is treated as the maximum value of your stored property [[co-1015]].
- **No living in units:** no occupant may use a self-service storage facility for residential purposes [[co-101]].
- **Warehouses:** if the owner issues a warehouse receipt, bill of lading or other document of title for your property, the Uniform Commercial Code applies instead of this article [[co-101]].
- **Other liens:** the article doesn't limit liens created by special contract or other laws [[co-art]].
- **Rent increases:** the article doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[co-art]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
