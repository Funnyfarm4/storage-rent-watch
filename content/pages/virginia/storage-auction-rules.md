Virginia's self-storage law is the **Virginia Self-Service Storage Act**, Code of Virginia Title 55.1, Chapter 29 (§§ 55.1-2900 to 55.1-2906) [[va-ch]]. The sale process is in § 55.1-2902 [[va-2902]]. In Virginia law the renter is called the **occupant**, and "owner" includes the operator, lessor, sublessor, agent or anyone authorized to manage the facility or collect rent [[va-2900]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement matters too, and the owner may use other legal remedies the Act doesn't displace [[va-2903]].

## The owner's lien

The owner has a lien on **all personal property stored in your leased space** for rent, labor or other charges, and for expenses reasonably incurred selling it under this chapter. The lien attaches when the property is stored [[va-2901]].

While the property remains in the leased space, that lien is **superior to other existing liens or security interests only up to $250**, or **$500 if the leased space is climate-controlled**. The lien also extends to any sale proceeds left after perfected liens are paid, and the owner may keep those proceeds until the rest of its charges are paid [[va-2901]].

Your rental agreement **must** contain a statement, **in bold type**, telling you the lien exists and that property in the space may be sold to satisfy it if you're in default [[va-2901]]. Unless the agreement says otherwise, exclusive care, custody and control of what's in the space stays with you [[va-2904]].

The owner of a self-service storage facility is **not** a warehouseman under Title 8.7 unless it issues a warehouse receipt, bill of lading or other document of title [[va-2900]].

## Late fees and lockouts

Chapter 29 **does not set a late-fee cap** or a statutory late-fee formula, so check your rental agreement for any late charges [[va-ch]].

**Default** means failing to perform on time any obligation in the rental agreement or this chapter [[va-2900]]. Whenever you're in default, the owner may **deny you access** to the leased space [[va-2902]]. There is no separate waiting period before lockout in § 55.1-2902.

The Act doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so check your rental agreement [[va-ch]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: First default notice

If you're in default, the owner must notify you by **regular mail** at your last known address, or by electronic means if your rental agreement expressly allows it [[va-2902]]. Your last known address is the street or email address in your rental agreement, or a later written change-of-address notice given the way the agreement requires [[va-2900]].

If the default **isn't cured within 10 days after it occurs**, the owner may proceed to enforce the lien by selling the contents of your unit at a **public auction for cash** [[va-2902]]. Before that auction, it still has to send the further notice in Step 2.

Your agreement may let you name an **alternative contact** to receive the notices in this section. Refusing to name one doesn't change your rights, and that contact has no right to access the space or your property unless the agreement says so [[va-2902]].

### Step 2: Further notice before the auction

After you've been in default for **10 days**, and before the owner can sell your property under this chapter, it must send a further notice of default by **verified mail**, postage prepaid, to your last known address — or by electronic means if the agreement expressly allows it and the sender keeps proof of delivery (an electronic receipt, a fax confirmation, or a certificate of service) [[va-2902]]. "Verified mail" means any USPS or private delivery method that provides evidence of mailing [[va-2900]].

That further notice must include [[va-2902]]:

- an itemized statement of the owner's claim, with the charges due on the date of the notice and the date they became due
- a demand for payment within a specified time **not less than 20 days after the date of the notice**
- a statement that the contents of your leased space are subject to the owner's lien
- a **conspicuous** statement that unless you pay in time, the contents will be sold at public auction at a specified time and place
- the name, street address and telephone number of the owner or designated agent you can contact

A notice is presumed delivered when it's properly addressed and deposited with the Postal Service with postage prepaid, or when it's sent electronically with the required proof of delivery. In a dispute, the sender has the burden to show delivery [[va-2902]].

> **Other lienholders:** if personal property has a fair market value over **$1,000** and a creditor has filed a financing statement in your name (at the State Corporation Commission or in the locality where the facility is, or where your last known address is), or if it's a registered watercraft with a Department of Wildlife Resources title lien, the owner must also notify that lienholder of record by **certified mail** at least **10 days** before the proposed auction [[va-2902]].

### Step 3: The public auction

Chapter 29 does **not** require a newspaper ad or other public advertisement beyond the notices above [[va-2902]]. The auction must be a public auction for cash, and it may be held [[va-2902]]:

1. at the self-service storage facility
2. at the nearest suitable place to where the property is held or stored, or
3. **online**

A good-faith buyer takes the property free and clear of the rights of people against whom the lien was valid [[va-2902]]. So it matters to act before the sale date.

> **Cars:** while a motor vehicle remains in the leased space, the owner also has a lien under the separate keeper-of-vehicles statute, Va. Code § 46.2-644.01, which expressly includes self-storage facilities [[va-2901]] [[va-vehicle]]. That statute has its own notice and reclaim rules (including checking the title for existing liens and a Department of Motor Vehicles notice process) [[va-vehicle]]. Unauthorized or trespassing vehicles or watercraft may be towed under local private-property towing ordinances [[va-2901]].

> **Boats:** if you've been in default for **more than 60 days**, the owner may have a watercraft **towed** instead of selling it. It must send notice by verified mail or email to your last known address at least **10 days** before the tow date, naming the tow company (this can be part of the further notice in Step 2). After the tow company takes the watercraft, the owner is immune from civil liability for damage that happens afterward [[va-2902]].

## How to stop the sale

At any time **before the public auction**, you may pay the amount necessary to satisfy the lien and **redeem** the personal property [[va-2902]]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, and ask for a receipt and written confirmation that the auction is cancelled.

Chapter 29 doesn't say a partial payment stops the auction, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the amount looks wrong, answer the notice using the contact details it must include [[va-2902]], in writing if you can. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

From the auction proceeds, the owner may satisfy its lien and must **hold any balance for delivery on demand** to you or to another lienholder referred to in the chapter. It does **not** have to keep holding a balance for a notified lienholder of record (or other lien creditor) that fails to claim an interest within **30 days** of the public auction [[va-2902]].

So long as the owner complies with Chapter 29, its liability to you is limited to the **net proceeds** received from the auction of your personal property [[va-2902]].

Chapter 29 does **not** set a specific number of years after which leftover money automatically goes to the state [[va-2902]]. Ask the facility in writing for the sale results and any balance as soon as you learn of a sale, and make sure it has your current address. If money later sits unclaimed, you can search for it on [vaMoneySearch.gov](https://www.vamoneysearch.gov/), the Virginia Department of the Treasury's official unclaimed property site [[va-up]] [[va-up-trs]].

## If the rental ends (and you still owe nothing for rent)

Separate from a money default, if personal property is left in the space **after termination or nonrenewal** of the rental agreement, the owner may treat it as abandoned. Before disposing of it, the owner must give you written notice of the termination or nonrenewal date and state that it will dispose of leftover property within **24 hours after a 10-day period** from that notice. You may remove the property at any reasonable time (subject to reasonable restrictions) until it's disposed of [[va-2903-1]].

That abandoned-property section **does not apply** when the default is a failure to meet a financial obligation; then §§ 55.1-2901 and 55.1-2902 (or other available remedies) apply instead [[va-2903-1]].

## If the owner broke the rules

Chapter 29 doesn't create a special statutory damages amount for noncompliance the way some other states do. The owner may also use other legal remedies the Act doesn't limit [[va-2903]]. To complain about a facility, you can contact the Virginia Attorney General's consumer protection team [[va-ag]].

## Other Virginia rules

- **No living in units:** no occupant may use a self-service storage facility for residential purposes [[va-2900]].
- **Rent increases:** Chapter 29 doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[va-ch]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
