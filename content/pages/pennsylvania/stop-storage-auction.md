If your Pennsylvania storage unit is headed for a lien sale, you usually still have time to act. Pennsylvania's self-storage rules are now in **Title 12, Chapter 56** of the Consolidated Statutes, added by Act 51 of 2025 [[pa-act51]]. The law calls the renter the **occupant** and the facility the **owner** [[pa-5602]]. Here are the steps, in order, based on the statute text. Read your own rental agreement and notice too.

## Step 1: Know where you are on the timeline

- After you've been in default **continuously for at least 10 days**, the owner may lock you out of your unit [[pa-5606]].
- The owner can't start enforcing its lien until you've been in default **continuously for at least 30 days**. Then it may also move your things to other suitable storage while a sale is pending [[pa-5606]].
- A motor vehicle, trailer or watercraft can be **towed after 60 days** of continuous default [[pa-5606]].

"Default" means not doing something on time that the chapter or your rental agreement requires, like paying rent [[pa-5602]]. If you can pay before the 30-day mark, do it and get a receipt.

## Step 2: Find the key dates on your notice

The owner must give you written notice of the default. It must include [[pa-5607]]:

- an itemized statement of the amount due and when it became due
- a demand for payment within a stated time of **at least 30 days after the notice date**
- a statement that the contents of your unit are subject to the owner's lien
- the name, street address and phone number of the owner or its agent you can contact
- a **bold**, conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time and at the stated place, your property will be advertised for sale or otherwise disposed of at a stated time and place, **not less than 30 days** after the notice date
- a statement that you've been locked out or that your property was moved, if the owner did either

Write down the payment deadline and the sale date right away. After the payment deadline passes, the owner must advertise the sale, and the sale can't happen **sooner than 10 days after the first ad** or posting [[pa-5608]]. The sale must also match the terms in the notice [[pa-5611]].

## Step 3: Check how the notice reached you

The notice must go to your last known address by **personal service, verified mail, certified mail (return receipt requested) or email** [[pa-5607]]. Verified mail is any USPS or private delivery method that gives proof of mailing [[pa-5602]]. A mailed notice counts as served once it's mailed, properly addressed and paid [[pa-5607]].

Email only counts if your agreement (or a later change to it) says email is an authorized way to notify you, you affirmatively agreed to electronic contact and to tell the owner if your email changes, and that consent is **printed in bold or underlined** in the agreement [[pa-5607]]. Your last known address is the postal or email address in your agreement, or one you later gave in a written change-of-address notice [[pa-5602]], so keep it current in writing. If something about the notice looks wrong, write down what you received and when, and contact free legal help.

## Step 4: Check the amount, especially late fees

The lien covers rent, labor, late fees and other storage charges, plus expenses to preserve your property and reasonable costs of selling it [[pa-5605]]. Compare every line on the itemized statement with your rental agreement.

Late fees have specific rules [[pa-5605]]:

- The owner can't charge a late fee unless the rental agreement discloses the amount and when it's charged.
- A late fee of **$20 a month or 20% of monthly rent, whichever is greater**, is treated as reasonable.
- No late fee may be charged if you pay rent and other charges in full **by the fifth day** after the due date.

Ask in writing about anything you don't recognize, and keep a copy.

## Step 5: Pay before the sale and get it in writing

Before the sale or other disposition, you can pay the amount needed to satisfy the owner's lien plus other reasonable expenses under the chapter and **redeem your property**. Once you do, the owner must give your property back [[pa-5610]].

1. Ask for an itemized payoff amount in writing.
2. Pay in a way that leaves a record.
3. Get a receipt saying the lien is paid in full, plus written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

Chapter 56 doesn't require the owner to accept a partial payment. Some facilities may agree to a payment plan or a short delay, but that's up to them, so get any deal in writing with the new deadline.

Don't wait for the auction day. A good-faith buyer at the sale gets the property free of your rights **even if the owner didn't follow the chapter** [[pa-5612]], so acting before the sale is what protects your things.

## Step 6: If the bill or notice is wrong

Chapter 56 doesn't set out a special court process for disputing the lien. If you think the amount is wrong, the notice didn't follow the rules, or the owner won't return your property after you pay, get free legal help right away. A lawyer can tell you your options.

You can also [submit a complaint to the Pennsylvania Attorney General](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/submit-a-complaint/) [[pa-ag]].

## After a sale: claim the leftover money

The owner may pay its lien from the sale proceeds but must hold any balance **for delivery to you on demand** [[pa-5614]]. Ask the facility in writing what the sale brought in, what it deducted, and how to get the balance.

If you don't claim it **within six months** of the sale, the balance is treated as abandoned and unclaimed property, and the owner must report and pay it to the **State Treasurer** [[pa-5614]]. After that, search your name on Pennsylvania's official [unclaimed property site](https://www.searchandclaimpa.gov/) [[pa-treasury]].

If the advertised sale didn't go through, the owner must tell you in writing about any other way it disposed of your property [[pa-5611]].

## Things that weren't yours

If you stored someone else's property, your rental agreement should ask you to tell the owner about it. The owner isn't liable for selling property that wasn't yours unless you told it so, and a value limit in your agreement is binding if it's printed in bold or underlined [[pa-5616]].
