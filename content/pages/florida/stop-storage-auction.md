If your Florida storage unit is behind and you've gotten a notice that your things will be sold, you can still stop the sale. Florida's Self-storage Facility Act, sections 83.801 to 83.809 of the Florida Statutes [[fl-ch]], gives the facility a lien on what's stored for rent and related charges [[fl-805]], but it also lets you **redeem** your property by paying before the sale [[fl-806]]. Here's how to use that right.

## Step 1: Find the key dates on your notice

To sell, the facility must give you written notice, delivered in person, by email, or by first-class mail with a certificate of mailing to your last known address, **and** conspicuously posted at the facility or on your unit [[fl-806]]. The notice must include [[fl-806]]:

- an itemized statement of the claim, showing the amount due and the date it became due
- a description of your property matching (or reasonably similar to) the one in your rental agreement
- a demand for payment within a stated time of **at least 14 days** after delivery
- a conspicuous statement that, unless you pay in time, your property will be advertised and sold or otherwise disposed of at a specified time and place
- the owner's name, street address and phone number so you can respond

Count from delivery. Mailed notice is presumed delivered when it's deposited with the Postal Service, properly addressed with postage prepaid [[fl-806]]. After your deadline passes, the sale must be advertised **once a week for 2 consecutive weeks** in a local newspaper of general circulation, and the sale can't happen until **at least 15 days after the first ad** [[fl-806]]. If there's no such newspaper, the ad is instead posted in at least three conspicuous places in the neighborhood at least **10 days** before the sale [[fl-806]]. The ad must list the time, place and manner of the sale, so look for it to learn the exact date [[fl-806]].

## Step 2: Check how the notice reached you

Your last known address is the street or P.O. box address in your latest rental agreement or in a later written change-of-address notice you gave by hand, first-class mail or email [[fl-803]]. If the facility only emailed a notice of the pending sale and got no response, return receipt or delivery confirmation from your email address, it must also send the notice by first-class mail with a certificate of mailing before going ahead with the sale [[fl-806]]. If something looks wrong, write down what you received and when, and contact free legal help.

## Step 3: Check the amount

Ask for an itemized payoff in writing if your notice doesn't show one [[fl-806]]. A late fee must be stated in your rental agreement or an addendum. Florida treats a late fee of **$20 or 20% of monthly rent, whichever is greater,** as reasonable, and the facility may also charge a reasonable fee for rent-collection or lien-enforcement expenses [[fl-808]]. Compare each charge with your agreement and ask about anything you don't recognize.

## Step 4: Pay before the sale and get it in writing

Before any sale or other disposition, you can pay the amount needed to satisfy the lien plus the reasonable expenses the facility incurred enforcing it. Once it receives that payment, the facility must return your property [[fl-806]]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, get a receipt saying the lien is paid in full, and ask for written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

Don't wait for auction day. A buyer in good faith takes the property free of your claims **even if the facility didn't follow the rules** [[fl-806]]. And if you don't redeem or pay the lien, the law treats you as having abandoned the unit, so the facility can take the space back [[fl-806]].

## Step 5: If you can't pay it all

The redemption right in the statute is for the full lien amount plus reasonable expenses [[fl-806]]. Some facilities may agree to a payment plan or a short delay, but that's up to them. If you reach a deal, get it in writing with the new deadline. If the sale date is close and you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) the same day.

## Step 6: Tell them if you're in the military

Florida requires every storage rental agreement or application to disclose whether you're a member of the uniformed services [[fl-808]]. If you are, make sure the facility knows, and ask a legal aid office about your federal protections before the sale date.

## Cars, trucks and boats

If rent and other charges tied to a motor vehicle or watercraft stay unpaid for **60 days**, the facility may sell it under the same rules or have it towed. Once a wrecker takes it, the facility isn't liable for it, and the towing company must follow Florida's separate towing notice and sale law, section 713.78 [[fl-806]]. If your vehicle or boat was towed, contact the towing company quickly.

## After a sale: claim the leftover money

The facility pays its lien from the sale money. Anything left over is held by the facility **for delivery to you on demand**, and it must send you notice of the balance in person or by first-class mail with a certificate of mailing. If you don't claim it within **2 years after the sale**, it's treated as abandoned and the facility no longer owes it to you [[fl-806]]. Florida's storage law doesn't send this money to the state's unclaimed property office, so ask the facility directly, in writing, and keep a copy. If a secured lender's lien comes ahead of the facility's, the money is held for that lender first [[fl-806]].

## Watch for value limits

If your rental agreement limits the value of what you can store, that limit is treated as the maximum value of your property [[fl-806]]. Keep that in mind if you're weighing whether to dispute a sale.
