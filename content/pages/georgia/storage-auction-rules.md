Georgia's self-storage law is the Georgia Self-service Storage Facility Act of 2013, O.C.G.A. §§ 10-4-210 to 10-4-218 [[ga-210]] [[ga-act]]. The sale process is in § 10-4-213, which spells it out as required wording for your rental agreement [[ga-213]]. In Georgia law the renter is called the **occupant** [[ga-211]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement can add terms of its own [[ga-214]], so read it too.

## The owner's lien

The facility has a lien on all personal property at the facility for rent, late fees, labor or other charges related to the property, plus expenses needed to preserve it and expenses reasonably incurred selling or disposing of it. The lien attaches the day your property is brought to the facility [[ga-212]].

"Personal property" includes vehicles, trailers, watercraft, household items and furnishings [[ga-211]].

## When you're in default

Under the required agreement wording, you're in **default** if a monthly payment isn't made by the **seventh calendar day** of the month it's due, or if your check bounces. Once you're in default, the owner may deny you access to your unit, without notice, until it gets paid [[ga-213]].

The owner can start the lien sale process only after you've been in default **continuously for 30 days** [[ga-213]].

If you never signed a written agreement the owner delivered by hand or verified mail, continuing to use the unit for 14 days can count as accepting it, but only if the agreement carries a specific bold-type warning saying so [[ga-218]].

## Late fees

If your rental agreement allows it, the owner can charge a late fee of no more than **$20 per month or 20% of the monthly rent per month, whichever is greater**, for each month a payment is late. This cap applies to agreements entered into, extended or renewed, and late payments, on or after July 1, 2019 [[ga-217]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: The lien notice

The notice must be in writing and delivered in person, by verified mail or by email [[ga-213]]. "Verified mail" means certified, registered or overnight delivery, or another method where the post office or delivery service gives proof the item was sent [[ga-211]]. The owner must also notify anyone with a superior lien on the property, such as a lender you disclosed [[ga-213]].

If the owner emails the notice and gets neither a non-automated reply nor a delivery receipt, it must send the notice by verified mail to your last known address (or your designated agent's) before going ahead with the sale [[ga-213]]. Your last known address is the street, PO box or email address in your latest rental agreement, or one you later gave in a written change-of-address notice [[ga-211]], so keep it current.

The notice must [[ga-213]]:

- include an itemized statement of the claim, showing the amount due and the date it became due
- tell you that you've been denied access to your property
- give the name, street address, email address and phone number of the owner or its agent you can contact
- demand payment within a set time **not less than 14 days** after delivery
- state that unless you pay in time, the property will be advertised and sold at a public sale to the highest bidder at a specified time and place

### Step 2: Advertising the sale

After the notice deadline passes, the owner must advertise the sale **once** in the legal organ for the county where the facility is located. For anything other than a motor vehicle, motorcycle, trailer, watercraft or recreational vehicle, the ad may instead be done in any other commercially reasonable way [[ga-213]].

The ad must include a brief general description of the property, the facility's address, the unit number if any, your name, and the time, place and manner of the sale [[ga-213]].

> **What changed in 2025:** Georgia used to require ads once a week for two consecutive weeks, with the sale at least 15 days after the *first* ad. A 2025 law cut this to a single ad, with the sale at least 15 days after publication, for lien enforcement started on or after July 1, 2025 [[ga-213]]. Older guides online may still describe the two-week rule.

### Step 3: The sale

The sale can't take place **sooner than 15 days after publication** [[ga-213]]. It must be held at the facility or the nearest suitable place, and a public sale includes offering the property on a publicly accessible website that regularly runs online auctions [[ga-213]]. If at least 3 independent bidders attend, the ad and the sale are deemed commercially reasonable [[ga-213]].

If no one buys the property, and the owner followed the rules, it may dispose of the property another way, and it must tell you what it did [[ga-213]]. A good-faith buyer takes the property even if the owner didn't follow every rule [[ga-213]], which is why it matters to act before the sale date.

> **Vehicles, trailers and boats:** if rent and other charges stay unpaid for 60 days after they were due, the owner may have the vehicle, trailer or watercraft towed instead of selling it, and isn't liable for it once the tow company takes it [[ga-213]].

> **Military renters:** if the rental agreement is with a service member, the owner must follow the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act [[ga-214]].

## How to stop the sale

Before any sale or other disposition, you can pay the amount needed to satisfy the lien plus the reasonable expenses incurred, and redeem your property [[ga-213]]. Pay in a way that gives you a receipt, and ask for written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

If you think the amount is wrong, compare it with the itemized statement in your notice and the late-fee cap above [[ga-213]] [[ga-217]]. If the sale date is close and you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

The owner can pay itself what it's owed from the sale proceeds, but must hold any balance for you (or a secured lender it notified). If the balance isn't claimed **within two years of the sale**, it's handled under Georgia's Disposition of Unclaimed Property Act [[ga-213]]. The Georgia Department of Revenue runs the state's unclaimed property program [[ga-dor]], and you can search for money in your name on its official [Georgia Unclaimed Property Search](https://gaclaims.unclaimedproperty.com/) site [[ga-claims]].

Don't wait two years: ask the facility in writing for the sale results and your balance as soon as you learn your unit was sold. Under the required agreement wording, the owner's liability can't exceed the sale proceeds, and if your agreement limits the value you may store, that limit is treated as the maximum value of your property [[ga-213]].

To complain about a facility, you can contact the Georgia Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division [[ga-ag]].

## If the facility ends your rental

The owner can end or not renew your rental agreement by written notice delivered by hand or verified mail. The notice must give you **at least 14 days** after delivery to remove everything. Until then the owner can limit access to removing your things during normal business hours, and it may dispose of anything left after the date in the notice [[ga-218]].

## Other Georgia rules

- **No living in units:** no occupant may use a storage facility for residential purposes [[ga-216]].
- **Rent increases:** the Act doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[ga-act]] [[ga-214]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
