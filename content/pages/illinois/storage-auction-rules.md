Illinois's self-storage law is the Self-Service Storage Facility Act, 770 ILCS 95 [[il-act]]. The lien sale process is in Section 4 [[il-4]]. In Illinois law the renter is called the **occupant** [[il-2]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement can add terms of its own, so read it too.

## The owner's lien

The facility has a lien on everything you store for rent, labor or other charges related to the property, plus expenses needed to preserve it and expenses reasonably incurred selling or disposing of it under the Act. The lien attaches the day your property is brought to the facility [[il-3]].

"Default" means failing to do anything your rental agreement or the Act requires [[il-2]]. If you never signed a written agreement the owner gave you, continuing to use the space counts as accepting it [[il-2]].

## Late fees

A late fee can only be charged if rent stays unpaid for **at least 5 days** after the due date in your agreement, and only if the fee amount and conditions are written in the agreement or an addendum. A late fee of $20 or 20% of the monthly rent, whichever is greater, is deemed reasonable [[il-710]]. The owner can also charge reasonable rent collection and lien enforcement expenses, but they must be shown on an **itemized list** available to you [[il-710]]. Ask for that list if your balance looks higher than expected.

## Lockout

If your rental agreement allows it, the owner can deny you access, for example by over-locking your unit. Until a sale, your property stays in your care and control, and rent and other charges keep adding up while you're locked out [[il-4]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: The lien notice

The notice must be delivered in person, or sent by verified mail (any mail service that gives evidence of mailing) or by email to your last known address [[il-4]] [[il-2]]. Your last known address is the mailing or email address in your latest rental agreement, or one you later gave the owner in a written change-of-address notice [[il-2]], so keep it up to date.

A mailed notice counts as delivered when it's deposited with the Postal Service, properly addressed and with postage paid. An email counts as delivered when the owner gets a delivery receipt; if no receipt comes back, the notice counts as delivered when it's sent by verified mail to your last known mailing address [[il-4]].

The notice must include [[il-4]]:

- an itemized statement of the claim, showing the amount due and the date it became due
- the facility's name, address and phone number, the date, time, location and manner of the sale (including the bidding website if the sale is online), and your name and unit number
- if you've been denied access, the name, street address and phone number of the person you can contact to respond
- a demand for payment within a time **not less than 14 days** after delivery
- a conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time, the property will be advertised and sold or otherwise disposed of at a specified time and place

### Step 2: Advertising the sale

After the notice deadline passes, the sale must be advertised **once a week for two consecutive weeks** in a newspaper of general circulation where the facility is located. The ad must give the facility's name, address and phone number, the date, time, location and manner of sale, and your name and unit number [[il-4]].

If there's no such newspaper where the facility is located, the ad must instead be posted in at least 6 conspicuous places in the neighborhood at least 10 days before the sale [[il-4]].

### Step 3: The sale

The sale can't take place **sooner than 15 days after the first publication** [[il-4]]. It must match the terms of the notice, and it must be held at the facility or the nearest suitable place; a sale on a publicly accessible website counts as held at the facility [[il-4]]. If 3 or more bidders unrelated to the owner attend, the sale and its proceeds are deemed commercially reasonable [[il-4]]. A good-faith buyer gets the property even if the owner didn't follow every rule [[il-4]], which is why it matters to act before the sale date.

> **Vehicles, trailers and boats:** if charges stay unpaid, or you're in default, for 60 days, the owner may have the vehicle, trailer or watercraft towed. Before selling a titled item like a vehicle or boat, the owner must check with the Secretary of State and notify every title holder or lienholder it finds [[il-4]].

## How to stop the sale

Any time **before** the sale, you can pay the amount needed to satisfy the lien plus the reasonable expenses incurred under the Act, and redeem your property. Once the owner gets that payment, it must return your property [[il-4]]. Get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

If you think the amount is wrong, ask for the itemized statement and the list of collection expenses [[il-4]] [[il-710]]. If the sale date is close and you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

The owner can pay itself what it's owed from the sale proceeds, but must hold any balance for **delivery to you on demand**. Illinois is stricter than many states here: if you don't claim the balance **within one year of the sale**, it becomes the owner's property and you lose the right to it [[il-4]]. Unlike some states, the Act doesn't send this money to a state unclaimed property office, so ask the facility directly, in writing, as soon as you learn your unit was sold.

To complain about a facility, you can contact the Illinois Attorney General's Consumer Protection division [[il-ag]].

## If the facility ends your rental

The owner can end or not renew your rental agreement by written notice delivered in person or by verified mail. The notice must give you **at least 14 days** after delivery to remove everything. Until then the owner can limit access to removing your things during normal business hours, and it may dispose of anything left after the date in the notice [[il-7]].

## Value limits in your lease

If your rental agreement caps the value of what you can store, the cap counts as the maximum value of your property, but only if that clause is printed in bold type or underlined [[il-75]]. Only the occupant named on the last rental agreement can sue for damages for a violation of the Act [[il-75]].
