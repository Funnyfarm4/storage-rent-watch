Minnesota's self-storage law is the **Minnesota Liens on Personal Property in Self-Service Storage Act, Minn. Stat. 514.970 to 514.979** [[mn-970]]. In this law the renter is the **occupant**, and the **owner** includes the owner, operator, lessor or sublessor, an agent, or anyone authorized to manage the facility or collect rent [[mn-971]]. You and the owner **can't waive or change** these rules by agreement [[mn-978]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement matters too.

## The owner's lien

The owner has a **lien on the personal property stored in your space** (or on the proceeds of that property in the owner's possession) for rent, labor and other charges specified in your rental agreement that have become due, plus expenses needed to preserve the property or reasonably incurred in selling or disposing of it [[mn-972]].

You're in **default** when you fail to pay the rent and other charges **within 15 days after they become due** under your rental agreement [[mn-971]]. The lien attaches on the date you're in default, unless you get a court order to recover your property [[mn-972]]. Two more limits matter:

- **Security deposit:** no lien is created if the owner holds a security deposit big enough to cover the rent and other charges at the time of the alleged default [[mn-972]].
- **Removal:** the owner loses the lien on property it lets you remove, or that it unjustifiably refuses to let you remove [[mn-972]].

Your rental agreement must disclose the owner's lien rights, including the right to deny access, and the extent and limits of any insurance the owner carries on your property. It can't excuse the owner from liability for damage caused by the owner's negligence [[mn-975]].

## Lockouts and the items you can always remove

On default, the owner must mail you a notice of default. It may deny you access only after **default, service of the notice, the denial-of-access date stated in the notice has passed, and any security deposit has been applied** to the unpaid rent [[mn-972]].

Even if you're locked out, before the sale you may remove certain items during ordinary business hours by presenting a list of them [[mn-972]]:

- **Personal papers and health aids**, for any occupant
- **Personal clothing** for you and your dependents, and **tools of the trade** you need to earn a living (up to **$125 market value per item**), if you show documentation from a government or nonprofit agency or legal aid office that you receive need-based relief (for example SNAP, medical assistance, MinnesotaCare or SSI), are eligible for legal aid, or are a survivor of domestic violence or sexual assault

If the owner unjustifiably refuses, you can ask a court for an order allowing access to remove those items, and the facility is liable for your costs, disbursements and attorney fees in getting it [[mn-972]].

## How notices are sent

Notices must be **delivered in person or sent by verified mail**, meaning any Postal Service or private delivery method that gives evidence of mailing [[mn-973]] [[mn-971]]. Verified mail is presumed delivered once it's properly addressed, stamped and deposited [[mn-973]]. Mailed notices go to the mailing address and any alternate address in your rental agreement, or to your last known address if the owner has reason to believe it's more current [[mn-974]].

**Email only with your consent.** The owner may send notices only by email if you gave informed, written consent, and only if your agreement contains a specific email-consent paragraph with a checkbox, in at least 12-point bold type if printed. You may withdraw that consent at any time. The owner must get an **electronic receipt** showing delivery to your email address; if it can't, it must deliver the notice in person or by verified mail, and your payment period **doesn't start until that delivery** [[mn-973]].

Your rental agreement must also let you name an **optional alternate contact person**, who is used only for the notice of default and gets no interest in your belongings [[mn-975]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: Default (15 days after rent is due)

Default starts when rent and other charges go unpaid **15 days after they're due** [[mn-971]].

### Step 2: Notice of default (at least 14 days to pay)

The owner must notify you, plus anyone who has given the owner written notice of a claim to an interest in the contents. For a registered motor vehicle or watercraft, the notice also goes to any lienholder or secured party on the title or known to claim an interest [[mn-973]]. The notice must include [[mn-973]]:

- the **amount owed** for rent and other charges and a **demand for payment within a stated time not less than 14 days after delivery**
- notice of **denial of access**, if your rental agreement permits it, and the **date** access will be denied
- a statement that access will be denied until the claim is satisfied, and that you can dispute the denial of access by **starting a court case**
- the **name, street address and phone number** of the owner or its agent you can contact
- a **conspicuous statement** that unless you pay in time, your property will be advertised for sale, with the **time and place of the sale**
- a **conspicuous list of the items you may remove without charge** (see above) if you're denied general access

### Step 3: Advertising the sale

After your payment deadline passes, the sale must be **published once a week for 2 consecutive weeks** in a newspaper of general circulation where the sale will be held. If there's no qualified newspaper there, the ad may be posted on an independent, publicly accessible website that advertises self-storage lien sales or public notices. The ad must give a general description of the goods, the name of the person whose account they're held on, and the time and place of the sale. If the lien is satisfied before the second ad, the second ad is waived [[mn-973]].

### Step 4: The sale

The sale can't take place **sooner than 45 days after default** (or **60 days** for a motor vehicle or watercraft), and **not sooner than 15 days after the first ad** [[mn-973]]. It must match the notice, be **public**, and be held either through an **online auction** or at the **storage facility** or nearest suitable place where the property is stored. All bidders, including online bidders, must register and agree to the rules of the sale, and the sale must be conducted in a **commercially reasonable** manner [[mn-973]].

> **Vehicles and boats:** the owner may sell a motor vehicle or watercraft under this procedure or have it **towed by a towing company**. If it's towed, the owner must send you the tower's name, address and phone number and the street address where you can redeem it. The owner's lien ends once the tower takes possession, and the owner isn't liable for the vehicle or for damage to it after that [[mn-973]].

## How to stop the sale

**Before the sale**, you (or anyone else entitled to the notice) may **redeem** your property by paying **an amount sufficient to satisfy the lien and the reasonable expenses** the owner incurred following the sale procedure. If enough is paid, **your property may not be sold** [[mn-973]]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, and ask for a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. The law doesn't say a partial payment stops the sale, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the amount looks wrong, call the contact listed in your notice [[mn-973]]. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

If you're hurt by a violation of these rules, you can bring a civil action for damages [[mn-973]]. If you go to court to get your property back, the case is heard in the county where the facility is, and the **owner** has to prove that you defaulted and that it followed the law [[mn-976]].

## Leftover money from the sale

The facility may pay its lien and reasonable expenses from the sale money, but must **hold any extra for you**, deliver it on demand, and notify you of your right to it. Any balance you don't claim for **more than one year after the sale** must be deposited into the **state's unclaimed property funds** [[mn-973]]. The Minnesota Department of Commerce runs the state's unclaimed property program [[mn-up]]; you can search for and claim money on its official site, [unclaimedproperty.mn.gov](https://unclaimedproperty.mn.gov/) [[mn-up-search]]. Don't wait: ask the facility in writing for your balance as soon as you can, and keep a copy.

## Other Minnesota rules

- **Eviction-style court case:** instead of a lien sale, the owner may sue to end your rental agreement, recover the space and dispose of your property. The first court appearance is set 7 to 14 days after the summons is issued (3 to 7 days in an expedited case about nuisance or dangerous behavior), and the summons must be served at least 7 days before the appearance [[mn-977]].
- **Who to contact:** before you move in, you must get in writing the name and address of the manager and of the owner or its agent for legal papers and notices, and that information must be posted at the facility [[mn-976]].
- **Value caps in the lease:** if your agreement limits the value of what you can store, that limit is the maximum value of your property if it's in bold or underlined. The limit can't be less than **$1,000** [[mn-975]].
- **Military status:** your rental agreement or application must ask whether you're in the uniformed services, the National Guard or a reserve component [[mn-975]].
- **No living in units:** an occupant must not use a self-service storage facility for residential purposes [[mn-971]].
- **Rent increases:** the act doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[mn-970]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
