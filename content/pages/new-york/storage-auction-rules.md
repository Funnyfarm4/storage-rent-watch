New York's self-storage lien rules are in **Lien Law section 182** [[ny-182]]. In it, the renter is called the **occupant**, the facility is the **owner** (which includes a manager or agent), and your rental contract is the **occupancy agreement** [[ny-182]]. Below is a plain-English summary of the statute text. Read your own agreement too.

## What your agreement must tell you

Before you move in, the facility must give you a dated, signed written agreement in at least 10-point bold type. It must list the actual monthly charge in dollars, an itemized list of every other charge (mandatory or optional) in dollars, the facility's business address and phone number, and any limit on the facility's liability for loss or damage [[ny-182]]. It must also carry two conspicuous notices: that the charges stated are the actual charges you must pay, and that you may choose to get late or lien notices by email by giving your email address in at least two places in the agreement [[ny-182]].

## The facility's lien

The facility has a lien on all personal property stored there for occupancy fees and other charges, expenses to preserve the property, reasonable costs of selling it, and other charges under the agreement. The lien attaches the day your property is brought in, and New York makes it **superior to any other lien or security interest** [[ny-182]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: The lien notice

The facility can enforce the lien by a public or private sale, but only after sending notice to everyone known to claim an interest in the goods [[ny-182]]. The notice must include [[ny-182]]:

- an itemized statement of the amount due
- a description of the property subject to the lien
- the nature of the proposed sale, and its time and place
- a demand for payment within a stated time of **at least 30 days from mailing** of the notice
- a conspicuous statement that if you don't pay in time, the goods will be advertised and sold in a commercially reasonable manner
- a statement that anyone claiming an interest can bring a court proceeding **within 10 days** of service if they dispute the lien or the amount

### Step 2: How the notice must be delivered

The notice must be personally delivered, sent by registered or certified mail to your last known address, or sent by **verified mail and email** to your last known address [[ny-182]]. Verified mail is any USPS or private delivery method that gives evidence of mailing, such as first-class mail with a certificate of mailing [[ny-182]].

Email only counts if your agreement says you consented to late or lien notices by email **and** you gave your email address in at least two places in it. An emailed notice is treated as delivered once a non-automated reply or a delivery receipt comes back [[ny-182]]. Your last known address is the street, PO box or email address you gave in the agreement or later under it, so keep it current in writing [[ny-182]].

### Step 3: Advertising and the sale

Section 182 says the notice must warn that the goods will be advertised and sold, and that the sale must be commercially reasonable [[ny-182]]. It doesn't spell out how the ads work. New York's general lien-sale article (Lien Law sections 200 to 204) covers liens on personal property in the lienor's possession, other than a few listed types such as warehouse and carrier liens [[ny-200]]. For property worth $100 or more, it requires a public auction in the city or town where the lien arose, advertised **once a week for 2 consecutive weeks** in a local newspaper, with the sale **at least 15 days after the first ad** [[ny-202]]. How these general rules fit together with section 182 for a particular sale is a question to put to a lawyer.

A sale isn't unreasonable just because a better price was possible at another time or place, but selling more goods than needed to cover the debt is generally not commercially reasonable [[ny-182]].

## How to stop the sale

### Option 1: Pay before the sale

Under the general lien-sale article, you can **redeem** at any time before the sale by paying the amount due on the lien plus legitimate expenses already spent serving the notice and advertising. You're then entitled to your property [[ny-203]]. Ask for an itemized payoff amount in writing, pay in a way that leaves a record, and get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

### Option 2: Challenge the lien in court within 10 days

If you think the facility isn't entitled to a lien, or the amount is wrong or more than the fair value of its services, you can bring a **special proceeding within 10 days** of being served the notice, in any court that could hear a claim for that amount. If you win, the court cancels or reduces the lien. If the facility wins in whole or part, the court fixes the amount and the sale may go ahead **5 days** after you're served the judgment and notice of entry, unless you redeem first [[ny-182]]. That deadline is short, so contact free legal help right away.

## If the facility won't give your things back

Refusing to return your goods after you pay the occupancy fees the law allows is an **unlawful detention** of goods. You can sue for damages and the return of your goods, and a judgment can be up to **three times your actual damages plus reasonable attorney fees**. The New York Attorney General can also ask a court to stop violations, and a court can impose a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation [[ny-182]]. You can [file a complaint with the Attorney General](https://ag.ny.gov/file-complaint) [[ny-ag]].

## Leftover money from the sale

Under the general lien-sale article, the facility keeps enough to pay its lien and the costs of advertising and sale. It holds any balance for you and must notify you that it's holding it. If nobody claims it **within 30 days of the sale**, the facility must deposit it with the treasurer or chamberlain of the city or village, the supervisor of the town, or in New York City the **Commissioner of Finance**, where the sale was held. That office pays it out on demand with satisfactory proof of identity. After **5 years** unclaimed, it becomes part of that local government's general funds [[ny-204]]. Ask the facility in writing what the sale brought in and where any balance went.

## Other New York rules to know

- **Charges must match your agreement:** the agreement must state the actual monthly charge and every other charge in dollars [[ny-182]]. Compare any late or lien bill against it.
- **Liability limits:** if your agreement caps what the facility owes for loss or damage, it must say you can ask in writing to raise that cap, show the rates for doing so, and include a request form [[ny-182]].
- **Rent increases:** section 182 has no rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls [[ny-182]]. See [My storage rent went up. What can I do?](/storage-rent-increase/)
