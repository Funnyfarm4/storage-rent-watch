If your New York storage unit is headed for a lien sale, you usually still have time to act. New York's self-storage rules are in **Lien Law section 182**, which calls the renter the **occupant** and the facility the **owner** [[ny-182]]. Here are the steps, in order, based on the statute text. Read your own occupancy agreement and notice too.

## Step 1: Find the key dates on your notice

Before a sale, the facility must send a notice to everyone known to claim an interest in the goods. It must include [[ny-182]]:

- an itemized statement of the amount due
- a description of the property subject to the lien
- the nature of the proposed sale and its time and place
- a demand for payment within a stated time of **at least 30 days from mailing** of the notice
- a conspicuous statement that if you don't pay in time, the goods will be advertised and sold
- a statement that you can bring a court proceeding **within 10 days** of service if you dispute the lien or the amount

Write down two dates right away: the 10-day deadline to go to court, and the payment deadline. New York's general lien-sale article also calls for the sale to be advertised once a week for 2 consecutive weeks in a local newspaper, with the sale at least 15 days after the first ad, for property worth $100 or more [[ny-202]]. Whether and how that applies to a particular self-storage sale is a question for a lawyer, but the date of sale in your notice is the one to beat.

## Step 2: Check how the notice reached you

The notice must be personally delivered, sent by registered or certified mail to your last known address, or sent by **verified mail plus email** [[ny-182]]. Verified mail means a mailing method that gives evidence of mailing, such as first-class mail with a certificate of mailing [[ny-182]].

Email only counts if your agreement says you consented to late or lien notices by email **and** you gave your email address in at least two places in it [[ny-182]]. Your last known address is the street, PO box or email address you gave in the agreement or later under it, so keep it current in writing [[ny-182]]. If something about the notice looks wrong, write down what you received and when, and contact free legal help.

## Step 3: Check the amount

Compare every charge on the itemized statement with your occupancy agreement. New York requires the agreement to list the actual monthly charge in dollars and an itemized list of every other charge that is or may be imposed, with each amount in dollars and whether it's mandatory or optional [[ny-182]]. The lien covers occupancy fees and other charges, expenses to preserve the property, reasonable costs of selling it, and other charges under the agreement [[ny-182]]. Ask in writing about anything you don't recognize.

## Step 4: If the bill is wrong, go to court within 10 days

If you think the facility isn't entitled to a lien, or part of the amount wasn't properly charged, you can bring a **special proceeding** within 10 days of being served the notice. It can be filed in any court that could enter a judgment for the amount of the lien [[ny-182]].

- If the court cancels the lien, you're entitled to your property once the judgment and notice of entry are served on the facility [[ny-182]].
- If the court finds part of the amount was wrong, it reduces the lien [[ny-182]].
- If the facility proves the lien, the judgment fixes the amount, and the sale can go ahead **5 days** after the judgment and notice of entry are served on you, unless you redeem first [[ny-182]].

The 10-day window is short, so contact free legal help (below) as soon as the notice arrives.

## Step 5: Pay before the sale and get it in writing

Under the general lien-sale article, you can **redeem** at any time before the sale by paying the amount due on the lien plus whatever legitimate expenses have already been spent serving the notice and advertising the sale. You're then entitled to your property [[ny-203]]. Ask for an itemized payoff in writing, pay in a way that leaves a record, and get a receipt saying the lien is paid in full and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

The statute doesn't require the facility to accept a partial payment. Some facilities may agree to a payment plan or a short delay, but that's up to them, so get any deal in writing with the new deadline.

## Step 6: If they won't give your things back

Once you pay the fees the law allows, refusing to hand over your goods is an **unlawful detention** under New York law [[ny-182]]. You can sue for damages and the return of your goods, and a judgment can be up to **three times your actual damages** plus reasonable attorney fees [[ny-182]]. The New York Attorney General can also ask a court to stop violations of section 182, and a court can impose a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation [[ny-182]]. You can [file a complaint with the Attorney General](https://ag.ny.gov/file-complaint) [[ny-ag]].

## After a sale: claim the leftover money

Under the general lien-sale article, the facility keeps enough of the sale money to cover the lien and the costs of advertising and sale. It holds the rest for you and must serve you a notice, personally or by mail, that it's holding the balance [[ny-204]]. If you don't claim it within **30 days of the sale**, the facility deposits it with the treasurer or chamberlain of the city or village, the **Commissioner of Finance in New York City**, or the town supervisor where the sale was held. That office pays it to you on demand with satisfactory proof of identity [[ny-204]]. After **5 years** unclaimed, it goes into that local government's general funds, so don't wait [[ny-204]].

Start by asking the facility in writing, and keep a copy. If more than 30 days have passed, ask the facility which office got the deposit; the facility has to file an affidavit with the deposit listing what was sold and the prices [[ny-204]].
