If your Ohio storage unit is headed for a lien sale, you usually still have time to act. Ohio's self-storage law is Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5322, and the sale rules are in section 5322.03 [[oh-03]]. The law calls the renter the **occupant** and the facility the **owner** [[oh-01]]. Here are the steps, in order, based on the statute text. Read your own rental agreement and notice too.

## Step 1: Find the key dates on your notice

Before a sale, the owner must send you a lien notice. It must include [[oh-03]]:

- your name and last known address
- an itemized statement of the amount due and the date it became due
- a brief, general description of the property
- if your agreement allows the owner to deny you access, the name, street address and phone number of the person you can contact to pay and get your property back or sign a new rental agreement
- a demand for payment within a stated time of **at least 10 days after the notice is delivered**
- a conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time, the property will be advertised and sold at auction, and may be sold privately or destroyed if no one buys it
- the street or internet address of the sale, if it won't be held at the facility

Write down the payment deadline right away. After it passes, the sale must be advertised once a week for **two consecutive weeks** in a county newspaper or in another commercially reasonable manner, and the sale must take place **at least 15 days after the first publication** [[oh-03]]. The sale must also match the terms in the notice [[oh-03]].

## Step 2: Check how the notice reached you

The notice must be delivered in person, or sent by certified mail, or by first-class mail or private delivery service with a certificate or verification of mailing, to your last known address [[oh-03]]. Your last known address is the mailing address in your most recent rental agreement, or one you later gave in a written change-of-address notice [[oh-01]], so keep it current in writing. A notice sent by first-class mail or private delivery counts as delivered once it's mailed, properly addressed and paid [[oh-03]].

Email is allowed only if you agreed to email notices and gave an email address in your original agreement or a later amendment, **and** the owner can show delivery with a response or return receipt. If delivery can't be shown, the owner must use one of the other methods. An emailed notice counts as delivered when the owner gets the response or return receipt [[oh-03]]. If something about the notice looks wrong, write down what you received and when, and contact free legal help.

## Step 3: Check the amount, especially late fees

The lien covers rent, labor, late fees and other charges in your rental agreement that have come due, plus expenses to preserve your property and reasonable costs of enforcing the lien or selling the property [[oh-02]]. Compare every line on the itemized statement with your rental agreement.

Late fees have specific rules [[oh-05]]:

- No late fee can be collected unless the amount and conditions are stated in the rental agreement or an addendum.
- No late fee can be charged if you pay rent in full **by the third day** after the due date.
- A late fee of **$20 or 20% of the late payment, whichever is greater**, is deemed reasonable. A higher fee is allowed only if it's reasonable, and the owner has to prove that.
- The owner can also charge reasonable expenses of collecting rent and enforcing the lien.

Ask in writing about anything you don't recognize, and keep a copy.

## Step 4: Pay before the sale, and plan the move

You can stop the sale by paying the amount needed to satisfy the owner's lien plus the reasonable expenses incurred under section 5322.03. In Ohio, once you pay, you must **immediately remove all your property** from the facility, unless the owner agrees to a new rental agreement [[oh-03]].

1. Ask for an itemized payoff amount in writing.
2. Ask in writing whether the facility will let you stay under a new rental agreement. If not, line up a truck, helpers and a new place before you pay.
3. Pay in a way that leaves a record.
4. Get a receipt saying the lien is paid in full, plus written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

Section 5322.03 doesn't require the owner to accept a partial payment. Some facilities may agree to a payment plan or a short delay, but that's up to them, so get any deal in writing with the new deadline.

Don't wait for the auction day. A good-faith buyer at the sale (other than the owner or its agent) takes the property free and clear, **even if the owner didn't follow the rules** [[oh-03]], so acting before the sale is what protects your things.

## Step 5: Cars, trailers and boats

A vehicle, trailer or boat can be towed at the owner's choice: 30 days after notice was sent to everyone holding a lien on it, after 60 days of unpaid rent and charges when no lienholders were identified, or before or after a sale of the other property in the unit [[oh-03]]. The notice to lienholders must name the towing company, and its name and street address must be given to you if you show the title or another document proving your interest [[oh-03]].

## Step 6: If the bill or notice is wrong

If the owner doesn't follow the sale requirements, it's liable for damages caused by that failure, and for conversion if the violation was willful [[oh-03]]. If you think the amount is wrong or the notice didn't follow the rules, get free legal help right away, before the sale date.

You can also [file a consumer complaint with the Ohio Attorney General](https://www.ohioattorneygeneral.gov/Individuals-and-Families/Consumers) [[oh-ag]].

## After a sale: claim the leftover money

The owner may pay its lien from the sale proceeds, but must mail any balance to your last known mailing address by certified mail, or by first-class mail or private delivery with proof of mailing. If the money comes back or your address isn't known, the owner must hold it for **two years** after the sale for delivery to you on demand [[oh-03]]. Ask the facility in writing what the sale brought in, what it deducted, and how to get the balance.

After two years, the balance becomes unclaimed funds under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 169 [[oh-03]]. Search your name with Ohio's [Division of Unclaimed Funds](https://unclaimedfunds.ohio.gov/) [[oh-unclaimed]].

## Value limits in your lease

If your rental agreement caps the value of what you can store, and that clause is in bold or underlined, the cap is the maximum value of your property. It can't be less than **$1,000**, and it can't limit your claim for loss caused by the owner's negligence [[oh-06]].
