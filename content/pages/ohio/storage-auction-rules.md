Ohio's self-storage law is Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5322, "Storage Facilities" [[oh-ch]]. The lien sale process is in section 5322.03 [[oh-03]]. In Ohio law the renter is called the **occupant** [[oh-01]]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement can add terms of its own, so read it too.

## The owner's lien

The facility has a lien on what you store for rent, labor, late fees and other charges in the rental agreement that have come due, plus expenses to preserve the property and reasonable expenses of enforcing the lien or selling the property [[oh-02]]. The lien attaches the day your property is brought to the facility [[oh-02]].

## Late fees

A "reasonable" late fee can be charged for each service period that rent isn't paid on time, but only if the amount and conditions are stated in the rental agreement or an addendum. **No late fee** can be charged if you pay in full by the third day after the due date. A late fee of $20 or 20% of the late payment, whichever is greater, is deemed reasonable; a higher one is allowed only if reasonable, and the owner must prove it. The owner can also charge reasonable rent collection and lien enforcement expenses [[oh-05]].

## The timeline, step by step

### Step 1: The lien notice

The notice goes to you and to anyone the owner knows claims an interest in the property, lienholders on any vehicle or boat, and anyone with a filed security interest [[oh-03]]. It must be delivered in person or sent by certified mail, or by first-class mail or private delivery with a certificate or verification of mailing, to the last known address. Email is allowed only if you agreed to it in writing in your agreement and the owner can show delivery with a response or return receipt [[oh-03]]. A mailed notice counts as delivered when it's mailed [[oh-03]].

The notice must include [[oh-03]]:

- your name and last known address
- an itemized statement of the claim, with the amount due and the date it became due
- a brief, general description of the property
- if you've been denied access, the name, street address and phone number of the person you can contact to pay
- a demand for payment within a time **not less than 10 days** after delivery
- a conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time, the property will be advertised and sold at auction, and may be sold privately or destroyed if no one buys it
- the street or internet address of the sale, if it won't be held at the facility

### Step 2: Advertising the sale

After the notice deadline, the sale must be advertised once a week for **two consecutive weeks** in a newspaper of general circulation in the county, or in any other commercially reasonable manner. Advertising counts as commercially reasonable if at least three independent bidders register for, view or attend the sale. The ad must describe the property, give your name and last known address, the facility's street address, and the time, place and manner of sale [[oh-03]].

### Step 3: The sale

The sale must take place **at least 15 days after the first publication** [[oh-03]]. It can be held at the facility or, if the notice listed the address, online or at the nearest suitable place, and it must match the terms of the notice [[oh-03]]. The owner may buy at the sale [[oh-03]]. If nobody buys, the owner may re-advertise, sell privately, or dispose of the property, including destroying it [[oh-03]].

> Vehicles, trailers and boats can be towed in some cases, for example 30 days after lienholders were notified, or after 60 days unpaid with no lienholders identified [[oh-03]].

## How to stop the sale

If you pay the amount needed to satisfy the lien plus the reasonable expenses of enforcing it, you must **immediately remove all your property** from the facility, unless the owner agrees to a new rental agreement [[oh-03]]. Get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. If the sale date is close and you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

## Leftover money from the sale

The owner can keep what it's owed from the sale proceeds, but must mail any balance to you at your last known mailing address by certified mail, or by first-class mail or private delivery with proof of mailing. If the money comes back or your address isn't known, the owner must hold it for **two years** after the sale for you to claim. After that it becomes unclaimed funds under Ohio Revised Code Chapter 169 [[oh-03]], which you can search for with the Ohio Department of Commerce's Division of Unclaimed Funds [[oh-unclaimed]].

If the owner fails to follow the sale requirements, it's liable for damages, and for conversion if the violation was willful [[oh-03]]. To complain about a facility, you can contact the Ohio Attorney General's consumer protection section [[oh-ag]].

## Value limits in your lease

If your rental agreement caps the value of what you can store, and that clause is in bold or underlined, the cap is the maximum value of your property. The cap can't be less than **$1,000**, and it can't limit claims for loss caused by the owner's negligence [[oh-06]].
