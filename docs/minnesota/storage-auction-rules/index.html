Minnesota Storage Unit Auction Rules (Minn. Stat. 514.970-514.979): Notices, Timeline and Your Rights

Minnesota storage auction rules: default after 15 days, a notice with 14 days to pay, no sale before day 45, two ads, stopping the sale and leftover money.

Quick answer

  • You're in default if rent and other charges aren't paid within 15 days after they're due under your rental agreement Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2].
  • The default notice must demand payment within a stated time not less than 14 days after delivery Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
  • No sale sooner than 45 days after default (60 days for a motor vehicle or watercraft) Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
  • The sale must be advertised once a week for 2 consecutive weeks, and it can't happen sooner than 15 days after the first ad Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
  • You can stop the sale any time before it happens by paying enough to satisfy the lien plus the owner's reasonable expenses Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
  • Leftover sale money unclaimed for more than 1 year goes to the state's unclaimed property account Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].

Minnesota's self-storage law is the Minnesota Liens on Personal Property in Self-Service Storage Act, Minn. Stat. 514.970 to 514.979 Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[1]. In this law the renter is the occupant, and the owner includes the owner, operator, lessor or sublessor, an agent, or anyone authorized to manage the facility or collect rent Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2]. You and the owner can't waive or change these rules by agreement Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[9]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text; your rental agreement matters too.

The owner's lien

The owner has a lien on the personal property stored in your space (or on the proceeds of that property in the owner's possession) for rent, labor and other charges specified in your rental agreement that have become due, plus expenses needed to preserve the property or reasonably incurred in selling or disposing of it Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].

You're in default when you fail to pay the rent and other charges within 15 days after they become due under your rental agreement Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2]. The lien attaches on the date you're in default, unless you get a court order to recover your property Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3]. Two more limits matter:

  • Security deposit: no lien is created if the owner holds a security deposit big enough to cover the rent and other charges at the time of the alleged default Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].
  • Removal: the owner loses the lien on property it lets you remove, or that it unjustifiably refuses to let you remove Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].

Your rental agreement must disclose the owner's lien rights, including the right to deny access, and the extent and limits of any insurance the owner carries on your property. It can't excuse the owner from liability for damage caused by the owner's negligence Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[6].

Lockouts and the items you can always remove

On default, the owner must mail you a notice of default. It may deny you access only after default, service of the notice, the denial-of-access date stated in the notice has passed, and any security deposit has been applied to the unpaid rent Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].

Even if you're locked out, before the sale you may remove certain items during ordinary business hours by presenting a list of them Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3]:

  • Personal papers and health aids, for any occupant
  • Personal clothing for you and your dependents, and tools of the trade you need to earn a living (up to $125 market value per item), if you show documentation from a government or nonprofit agency or legal aid office that you receive need-based relief (for example SNAP, medical assistance, MinnesotaCare or SSI), are eligible for legal aid, or are a survivor of domestic violence or sexual assault

If the owner unjustifiably refuses, you can ask a court for an order allowing access to remove those items, and the facility is liable for your costs, disbursements and attorney fees in getting it Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].

How notices are sent

Notices must be delivered in person or sent by verified mail, meaning any Postal Service or private delivery method that gives evidence of mailing Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4] Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2]. Verified mail is presumed delivered once it's properly addressed, stamped and deposited Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. Mailed notices go to the mailing address and any alternate address in your rental agreement, or to your last known address if the owner has reason to believe it's more current Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[5].

Email only with your consent. The owner may send notices only by email if you gave informed, written consent, and only if your agreement contains a specific email-consent paragraph with a checkbox, in at least 12-point bold type if printed. You may withdraw that consent at any time. The owner must get an electronic receipt showing delivery to your email address; if it can't, it must deliver the notice in person or by verified mail, and your payment period doesn't start until that delivery Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].

Your rental agreement must also let you name an optional alternate contact person, who is used only for the notice of default and gets no interest in your belongings Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[6].

The timeline, step by step

Step 1: Default (15 days after rent is due)

Default starts when rent and other charges go unpaid 15 days after they're due Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2].

Step 2: Notice of default (at least 14 days to pay)

The owner must notify you, plus anyone who has given the owner written notice of a claim to an interest in the contents. For a registered motor vehicle or watercraft, the notice also goes to any lienholder or secured party on the title or known to claim an interest Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. The notice must include Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]:

  • the amount owed for rent and other charges and a demand for payment within a stated time not less than 14 days after delivery
  • notice of denial of access, if your rental agreement permits it, and the date access will be denied
  • a statement that access will be denied until the claim is satisfied, and that you can dispute the denial of access by starting a court case
  • the name, street address and phone number of the owner or its agent you can contact
  • a conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time, your property will be advertised for sale, with the time and place of the sale
  • a conspicuous list of the items you may remove without charge (see above) if you're denied general access

Step 3: Advertising the sale

After your payment deadline passes, the sale must be published once a week for 2 consecutive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation where the sale will be held. If there's no qualified newspaper there, the ad may be posted on an independent, publicly accessible website that advertises self-storage lien sales or public notices. The ad must give a general description of the goods, the name of the person whose account they're held on, and the time and place of the sale. If the lien is satisfied before the second ad, the second ad is waived Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].

Step 4: The sale

The sale can't take place sooner than 45 days after default (or 60 days for a motor vehicle or watercraft), and not sooner than 15 days after the first ad Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. It must match the notice, be public, and be held either through an online auction or at the storage facility or nearest suitable place where the property is stored. All bidders, including online bidders, must register and agree to the rules of the sale, and the sale must be conducted in a commercially reasonable manner Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].

Vehicles and boats: the owner may sell a motor vehicle or watercraft under this procedure or have it towed by a towing company. If it's towed, the owner must send you the tower's name, address and phone number and the street address where you can redeem it. The owner's lien ends once the tower takes possession, and the owner isn't liable for the vehicle or for damage to it after that Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].

How to stop the sale

Before the sale, you (or anyone else entitled to the notice) may redeem your property by paying an amount sufficient to satisfy the lien and the reasonable expenses the owner incurred following the sale procedure. If enough is paid, your property may not be sold Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. Pay in a way that leaves a record, and ask for a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. The law doesn't say a partial payment stops the sale, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the amount looks wrong, call the contact listed in your notice Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

If you're hurt by a violation of these rules, you can bring a civil action for damages Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. If you go to court to get your property back, the case is heard in the county where the facility is, and the owner has to prove that you defaulted and that it followed the law Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[7].

Leftover money from the sale

The facility may pay its lien and reasonable expenses from the sale money, but must hold any extra for you, deliver it on demand, and notify you of your right to it. Any balance you don't claim for more than one year after the sale must be deposited into the state's unclaimed property funds Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. The Minnesota Department of Commerce runs the state's unclaimed property program Minnesota Department of Commerce[10]; you can search for and claim money on its official site, unclaimedproperty.mn.gov Minnesota Department of Commerce[11]. Don't wait: ask the facility in writing for your balance as soon as you can, and keep a copy.

Other Minnesota rules

  • Eviction-style court case: instead of a lien sale, the owner may sue to end your rental agreement, recover the space and dispose of your property. The first court appearance is set 7 to 14 days after the summons is issued (3 to 7 days in an expedited case about nuisance or dangerous behavior), and the summons must be served at least 7 days before the appearance Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[8].
  • Who to contact: before you move in, you must get in writing the name and address of the manager and of the owner or its agent for legal papers and notices, and that information must be posted at the facility Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[7].
  • Value caps in the lease: if your agreement limits the value of what you can store, that limit is the maximum value of your property if it's in bold or underlined. The limit can't be less than $1,000 Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[6].
  • Military status: your rental agreement or application must ask whether you're in the uniformed services, the National Guard or a reserve component Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[6].
  • No living in units: an occupant must not use a self-service storage facility for residential purposes Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2].
  • Rent increases: the act doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[1]. See My storage rent went up. What can I do?

Frequently asked questions

How long before a storage unit is auctioned in Minnesota?
The law sets minimums, not a fixed date. You're in default once rent and other charges go unpaid for 15 days after they're due Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[2]. The default notice must give you at least 14 days after delivery to pay, the sale can't be sooner than 45 days after default (60 days for a vehicle or boat), and it can't be sooner than 15 days after the first newspaper ad Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
Can a Minnesota storage facility send auction notices by email?
Only with your informed, written consent, and only if your rental agreement (or a written change to it) contains a specific email-consent paragraph with a checkbox, printed in at least 12-point bold type. You can withdraw consent at any time. The owner must get an electronic receipt showing the email was delivered; if it can't verify delivery, it must deliver the notice in person or by verified mail, and your payment period doesn't start until that happens Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
Can a Minnesota storage facility lock me out?
Only after you're in default, the owner has served the notice of default, the date for denial of access stated in the notice has passed, and any security deposit has been applied to the unpaid rent Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3]. The notice must say whether your rental agreement permits denial of access and the date it starts Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. Even then, you may remove personal papers and health aids on demand Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[3].
How do I stop a storage auction in Minnesota?
Before the sale, pay the amount sufficient to satisfy the lien and the reasonable expenses the owner incurred following the sale procedure. If enough is paid, your property may not be sold Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4]. Get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.
Can they tow my car or boat from a Minnesota storage unit?
Yes. If the lien is on a motor vehicle or watercraft, the owner may sell it under the lien procedure (no sooner than 60 days after default) or have it towed by a towing company. If it's towed, the owner must send you the towing company's name, address and phone number and the street address where you can redeem it. The owner's lien ends when the tower takes possession Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4].
What happens to the leftover money after a Minnesota storage auction?
The facility may pay its lien and reasonable expenses from the sale money, but must hold the rest for you on demand and notify you of your right to it. If you don't claim it for more than one year after the sale, it must be deposited with the state's unclaimed property funds Minnesota Office of the Revisor of Statutes[4], which the Minnesota Department of Commerce runs Minnesota Department of Commerce[10].
