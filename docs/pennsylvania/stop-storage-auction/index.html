How to Stop a Storage Unit Auction in Pennsylvania (and Claim Leftover Sale Money)

Steps to stop a Pennsylvania storage lien sale under 12 Pa.C.S. Ch. 56: check the 30-day notice and late fees, redeem before the sale, and claim leftover money.

If your Pennsylvania storage unit is headed for a lien sale, you usually still have time to act. Pennsylvania's self-storage rules are now in Title 12, Chapter 56 of the Consolidated Statutes, added by Act 51 of 2025 Pennsylvania General Assembly[1]. The law calls the renter the occupant and the facility the owner Pennsylvania General Assembly[2]. Here are the steps, in order, based on the statute text. Read your own rental agreement and notice too.

Step 1: Know where you are on the timeline

  • After you've been in default continuously for at least 10 days, the owner may lock you out of your unit Pennsylvania General Assembly[4].
  • The owner can't start enforcing its lien until you've been in default continuously for at least 30 days. Then it may also move your things to other suitable storage while a sale is pending Pennsylvania General Assembly[4].
  • A motor vehicle, trailer or watercraft can be towed after 60 days of continuous default Pennsylvania General Assembly[4].

"Default" means not doing something on time that the chapter or your rental agreement requires, like paying rent Pennsylvania General Assembly[2]. If you can pay before the 30-day mark, do it and get a receipt.

Step 2: Find the key dates on your notice

The owner must give you written notice of the default. It must include Pennsylvania General Assembly[5]:

  • an itemized statement of the amount due and when it became due
  • a demand for payment within a stated time of at least 30 days after the notice date
  • a statement that the contents of your unit are subject to the owner's lien
  • the name, street address and phone number of the owner or its agent you can contact
  • a bold, conspicuous statement that unless you pay in time and at the stated place, your property will be advertised for sale or otherwise disposed of at a stated time and place, not less than 30 days after the notice date
  • a statement that you've been locked out or that your property was moved, if the owner did either

Write down the payment deadline and the sale date right away. After the payment deadline passes, the owner must advertise the sale, and the sale can't happen sooner than 10 days after the first ad or posting Pennsylvania General Assembly[6]. The sale must also match the terms in the notice Pennsylvania General Assembly[8].

Step 3: Check how the notice reached you

The notice must go to your last known address by personal service, verified mail, certified mail (return receipt requested) or email Pennsylvania General Assembly[5]. Verified mail is any USPS or private delivery method that gives proof of mailing Pennsylvania General Assembly[2]. A mailed notice counts as served once it's mailed, properly addressed and paid Pennsylvania General Assembly[5].

Email only counts if your agreement (or a later change to it) says email is an authorized way to notify you, you affirmatively agreed to electronic contact and to tell the owner if your email changes, and that consent is printed in bold or underlined in the agreement Pennsylvania General Assembly[5]. Your last known address is the postal or email address in your agreement, or one you later gave in a written change-of-address notice Pennsylvania General Assembly[2], so keep it current in writing. If something about the notice looks wrong, write down what you received and when, and contact free legal help.

Step 4: Check the amount, especially late fees

The lien covers rent, labor, late fees and other storage charges, plus expenses to preserve your property and reasonable costs of selling it Pennsylvania General Assembly[3]. Compare every line on the itemized statement with your rental agreement.

Late fees have specific rules Pennsylvania General Assembly[3]:

  • The owner can't charge a late fee unless the rental agreement discloses the amount and when it's charged.
  • A late fee of $20 a month or 20% of monthly rent, whichever is greater, is treated as reasonable.
  • No late fee may be charged if you pay rent and other charges in full by the fifth day after the due date.

Ask in writing about anything you don't recognize, and keep a copy.

Step 5: Pay before the sale and get it in writing

Before the sale or other disposition, you can pay the amount needed to satisfy the owner's lien plus other reasonable expenses under the chapter and redeem your property. Once you do, the owner must give your property back Pennsylvania General Assembly[7].

  1. Ask for an itemized payoff amount in writing.
  2. Pay in a way that leaves a record.
  3. Get a receipt saying the lien is paid in full, plus written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

Chapter 56 doesn't require the owner to accept a partial payment. Some facilities may agree to a payment plan or a short delay, but that's up to them, so get any deal in writing with the new deadline.

Don't wait for the auction day. A good-faith buyer at the sale gets the property free of your rights even if the owner didn't follow the chapter Pennsylvania General Assembly[9], so acting before the sale is what protects your things.

Step 6: If the bill or notice is wrong

Chapter 56 doesn't set out a special court process for disputing the lien. If you think the amount is wrong, the notice didn't follow the rules, or the owner won't return your property after you pay, get free legal help right away. A lawyer can tell you your options.

After a sale: claim the leftover money

The owner may pay its lien from the sale proceeds but must hold any balance for delivery to you on demand Pennsylvania General Assembly[10]. Ask the facility in writing what the sale brought in, what it deducted, and how to get the balance.

If you don't claim it within six months of the sale, the balance is treated as abandoned and unclaimed property, and the owner must report and pay it to the State Treasurer Pennsylvania General Assembly[10]. After that, search your name on Pennsylvania's official unclaimed property site Pennsylvania Treasury[12].

If the advertised sale didn't go through, the owner must tell you in writing about any other way it disposed of your property Pennsylvania General Assembly[8].

Things that weren't yours

If you stored someone else's property, your rental agreement should ask you to tell the owner about it. The owner isn't liable for selling property that wasn't yours unless you told it so, and a value limit in your agreement is binding if it's printed in bold or underlined Pennsylvania General Assembly[11].

Frequently asked questions

How much time do I have to stop a storage auction in Pennsylvania?
The owner can't start enforcing its lien until you've been in default continuously for at least 30 days Pennsylvania General Assembly[4]. The written notice must then give you at least 30 days after the notice date to pay, and must say the property won't be sold or disposed of sooner than 30 days after that date Pennsylvania General Assembly[5]. The sale also can't happen sooner than 10 days after the first newspaper ad or posting Pennsylvania General Assembly[6]. You can redeem right up until the sale Pennsylvania General Assembly[7].
What do I have to pay to stop the sale?
The amount needed to satisfy the owner's lien plus other reasonable expenses incurred under Chapter 56 Pennsylvania General Assembly[7]. The lien covers rent, labor, late fees and other storage charges, plus expenses to preserve the property and reasonable costs of selling it Pennsylvania General Assembly[3]. The notice must include an itemized statement showing the sum due and when it became due Pennsylvania General Assembly[5], so compare it with your rental agreement.
Can a Pennsylvania storage facility charge a late fee?
Yes, but only if the rental agreement discloses the amount and when it's charged. A late fee of $20 a month or 20% of monthly rent, whichever is greater, is treated as reasonable, and no late fee may be charged if you pay rent and other charges in full by the fifth day after the due date Pennsylvania General Assembly[3].
Does an emailed default notice count in Pennsylvania?
Only if your rental agreement (or a later change to it) says email is an authorized way to notify you, you affirmatively agreed to electronic contact and to tell the owner if your email changes, and that consent is printed in bold or underlined in the agreement Pennsylvania General Assembly[5].
Where does leftover auction money go in Pennsylvania?
The owner pays its lien from the sale proceeds and holds any balance for delivery to you on demand. If you don't claim it within six months of the sale, it's treated as abandoned and unclaimed property, and the owner must report and pay it to the State Treasurer Pennsylvania General Assembly[10]. After that, search your name on Pennsylvania's official unclaimed property site Pennsylvania Treasury[12].
