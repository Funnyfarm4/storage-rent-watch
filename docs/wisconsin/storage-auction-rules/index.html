Wisconsin Storage Unit Auction Rules (Wis. Stat. 704.90): Notices, Timeline and Your Rights

Wisconsin storage auction rules: default after 7 days, two notices with 14+ days to pay, one newspaper ad, sale 15+ days later, late fees and leftover money.

  • Before a sale the operator must send 2 notices; the 2nd must demand payment within a time not sooner than 14 days after the date of the notice Wisconsin State Legislature[1].

Wisconsin's self-storage law is Wis. Stat. 704.90, Self-service storage facilities Wisconsin State Legislature[1]. In this law the renter is the lessee, and the operator is the owner, lessor or sublessor, an agent, or anyone authorized to manage the facility or collect rent. It also covers portable self-service storage units (a box, shipping container or trailer leased mainly for storage), whether kept at the company's lot or at a place you choose Wisconsin State Legislature[1]. Everything below is a plain-English summary of the statute text, cited by subsection; your rental agreement matters too.

The operator's lien

The operator has a lien on all personal property stored in your space for rent and other charges related to it, including expenses needed to preserve, remove, store, prepare for sale and sell the property. The lien attaches on the first day your property is stored there Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 3).

Your written rental agreement must Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (subs. 2m and 3):

  • state in boldface that the operator has a lien and may satisfy it by selling your property if you default
  • let you name another person and address the operator must also send the first notice to
  • print any limit on the value of what you may store in bold or underlined type the same size as the rest of the agreement; that limit is presumed to be the maximum value of your property

You're in default when you fail to pay rent or other charges for 7 consecutive days after the due date in your agreement Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 1).

Late fees

The operator may charge a reasonable late fee for each month you don't pay rent by 5 days after it's due, but only if the amount is in your rental agreement. A late fee of $20 or 20% of the monthly rent, whichever is greater, is presumed reasonable. The operator can charge more, but then it has the burden of proving the higher fee is reasonable Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 4b).

Lockouts

If you default, the operator may deny you access to your property until you redeem it by paying what's due Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 4r). The same rules apply if your rental agreement ended and you left property behind without paying storage rent: the operator may move it elsewhere or keep it where it is, charge reasonable rent for storing it, and use the same default procedure Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (subs. 4g and 4r).

The timeline, step by step

Step 1: Default (7 days after rent is due)

Default starts when rent or other charges go unpaid for 7 consecutive days after the due date Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 1).

Step 2: First notice

The operator must send a first notice by regular mail or email to your last-known address or email address, and to the person you named in your rental agreement, if any. It must include Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 5b):

  • notice that you're in default (or haven't paid for property left after the agreement ended)
  • notice of denial of access, if your agreement or the law permits it
  • the name, street address and phone number of the operator you can contact to redeem your property

Step 3: Second notice (at least 14 days to pay)

The operator must then send a second notice by verified mail (any postal or commercial mail service that provides evidence of mailing) to your last-known address, or by email. It must include Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (subs. 1 and 5b):

  • a statement that the operator has a lien on your stored property
  • notice of denial of access, if permitted
  • an itemized statement of the rent and other charges due now, plus the charges that will come due before the sale and when
  • a demand for payment within a time not sooner than 14 days after the date of the notice
  • a statement that unless you pay in time, your property may be disposed of (if worth less than $100), towed (if it's a vehicle) or sold, with the date, time and place of any sale
  • a statement that sale money first pays the lien, and any balance goes back to you, or to the Department of Revenue if you can't be found
  • the operator's name, street address and phone number

When it sends the second notice, the operator must have photos or a video of your property available, and must show them to you if you ask Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 5c).

Email rules: a notice may go by email only if the operator uses a service that confirms receipt and actually gets confirmation that the notice was received. Otherwise it must use regular or verified mail. A mailed notice is presumed delivered once it's properly addressed, stamped and deposited with the Postal Service Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 7). Keep your address and email current with the facility in writing; a written change-of-address notice becomes your last-known address Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 1am).

Step 4: Advertising the sale

After your payment deadline passes, the operator may sell only if the sale is advertised once in a newspaper of general circulation where the facility or unit is located. The ad must include a brief, general description of the property and the facility's (or the portable unit operator's) address and your name Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6a).

Step 5: The sale

The sale can't take place sooner than 15 days after the newspaper ad, and it must follow the terms of the notices Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6a). It must be one of these Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6a):

  • a public sale attended by 3 or more bidders, as one lot or several
  • a private sale after the property is offered to at least 3 people who deal in that type of property
  • another commercially reasonable method, including online

The sale must be on an internet site reasonably expected to attract bidders or, if in person, at the facility, at the unit, or at the nearest suitable place. If an in-person public sale is hit by bad weather, the operator may postpone it up to 14 days, but must run a new ad explaining the postponement and send you notice of the new date, time and place at least 5 days before it Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6am).

Low-value property: if your stored property's fair market value is less than $100, after the notices the operator may donate it to a charity, recycle it, take it to a solid waste facility or dispose of it another reasonable way instead of selling it Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 5m).

Vehicles: instead of selling a vehicle, the operator may have it towed if you were at least 7 days late, both notices were sent properly, you were more than 60 consecutive days behind before the 2nd notice was sent, and you didn't redeem by the deadline. The operator isn't liable for damage to or loss of the vehicle from the towing Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 5r).

How to stop the sale

At any time before the property is disposed of, towed or sold, you may redeem it by paying the operator the full amount of rent and all other charges due. Once the operator gets that payment, it must return your property Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 5a). Ask for the itemized amount in writing, pay in a way that leaves a record, and get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled. The law doesn't say a partial payment stops the sale, so if you agree on a payment plan, get it in writing. If the sale date is close or you can't pay, contact free legal help (below) right away.

A buyer in good faith gets your property free of your rights even if the operator broke the rules, so act before the sale Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6c).

Leftover money from the sale

The operator must first apply the sale money to the lien, then return any balance to you. If it can't locate you with due diligence, it must report and deliver the balance to the Wisconsin secretary of revenue under the state's unclaimed property law Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 6b). A Wisconsin Court of Appeals decision noted in the official statutes says nothing in the law lets the operator keep holding the extra money once you show up or contact it about it Wisconsin State Legislature[2]. Ask the facility in writing for your balance as soon as you can, and keep a copy.

If the money has already gone to the state, the Wisconsin Department of Revenue runs the unclaimed property program; you can search by name and file a claim online, with a copy of your government ID Wisconsin Department of Revenue[3].

If the operator breaks the rules

  • Sue for damages: a lessee hurt by a violation may bring a civil action for damages, costs, disbursements and reasonable attorney fees, plus other relief a court allows Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 12). The same court decision says a rental agreement can't take away reasonable attorney fees or compensatory damages under this law Wisconsin State Legislature[2].
  • Complain to the state: the Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection (DATCP) investigates alleged violations Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 11), and you can file a consumer complaint with it online Wisconsin Department of Agriculture, Trade and Consumer Protection[4].
  • Penalties: a violator may have to forfeit up to $1,000 for a first offense and up to $3,000 for a later offense within a year; each day of a continued violation is a separate offense Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 10).

Other Wisconsin rules

  • No living in units: you may not use a storage space for residential purposes, and the operator may not knowingly allow it Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 2).
  • Your property, your control: except as your agreement and the law provide, you have exclusive care, custody and control of what you store Wisconsin State Legislature[1] (sub. 4).
  • Rent increases: the law doesn't set a rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls Wisconsin State Legislature[1]. See My storage rent went up. What can I do?

Frequently asked questions

How long before a storage unit is auctioned in Wisconsin?
The law sets minimums, not a fixed date. You're in default once rent or other charges go unpaid for 7 consecutive days after the due date. The operator must then send two notices, and the second must give you at least 14 days from the date of the notice to pay. The sale can only happen after that deadline passes, and not sooner than 15 days after a newspaper ad Wisconsin State Legislature[1].
Can a Wisconsin storage facility send auction notices by email?
Yes, but only if the operator uses a service that confirms receipt of the email and actually gets confirmation that you received it. If it doesn't use such a service or receipt isn't confirmed, it must send the notice by regular mail (1st notice) or verified mail (2nd notice) instead Wisconsin State Legislature[1].
Can a Wisconsin storage facility lock me out?
Yes. Once you're in default, the operator may deny you access to your property until you redeem it by paying the full rent and other charges due Wisconsin State Legislature[1].
What is a legal late fee for a storage unit in Wisconsin?
The operator may charge a reasonable late fee for each month you don't pay rent by 5 days after it's due, but only if the amount is in your rental agreement. A late fee of $20 or 20% of the monthly rent, whichever is greater, is presumed reasonable. The operator may charge more, but then it has the burden of proving the higher fee is reasonable Wisconsin State Legislature[1].
Can they tow my car from a Wisconsin storage unit?
Yes, instead of selling it, if all of these apply: you were at least 7 days late, the operator sent both notices properly, you were more than 60 consecutive days behind before the 2nd notice was sent, and you didn't redeem in time. The operator isn't liable for damage or loss arising from the towing Wisconsin State Legislature[1].
What happens to the leftover money after a Wisconsin storage auction?
The operator first applies the sale money to its lien, then must return any balance to you. If it can't locate you with due diligence, it must report and deliver the balance to the Wisconsin Department of Revenue under the state's unclaimed property law Wisconsin State Legislature[1]. You can search and claim through the department's unclaimed property program Wisconsin Department of Revenue[3].
