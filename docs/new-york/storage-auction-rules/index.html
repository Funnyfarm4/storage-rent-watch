New York Storage Unit Auction Rules (Lien Law 182): Timeline, Notices and Your Rights

How a New York storage unit gets sold under Lien Law 182: the 30-day notice, email rules, the 10-day court challenge, redemption and leftover money.

New York's self-storage lien rules are in Lien Law section 182 Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. In it, the renter is called the occupant, the facility is the owner (which includes a manager or agent), and your rental contract is the occupancy agreement Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. Below is a plain-English summary of the statute text. Read your own agreement too.

What your agreement must tell you

Before you move in, the facility must give you a dated, signed written agreement in at least 10-point bold type. It must list the actual monthly charge in dollars, an itemized list of every other charge (mandatory or optional) in dollars, the facility's business address and phone number, and any limit on the facility's liability for loss or damage Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. It must also carry two conspicuous notices: that the charges stated are the actual charges you must pay, and that you may choose to get late or lien notices by email by giving your email address in at least two places in the agreement Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].

The facility's lien

The facility has a lien on all personal property stored there for occupancy fees and other charges, expenses to preserve the property, reasonable costs of selling it, and other charges under the agreement. The lien attaches the day your property is brought in, and New York makes it superior to any other lien or security interest Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].

The timeline, step by step

Step 1: The lien notice

The facility can enforce the lien by a public or private sale, but only after sending notice to everyone known to claim an interest in the goods Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. The notice must include Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]:

  • an itemized statement of the amount due
  • a description of the property subject to the lien
  • the nature of the proposed sale, and its time and place
  • a demand for payment within a stated time of at least 30 days from mailing of the notice
  • a conspicuous statement that if you don't pay in time, the goods will be advertised and sold in a commercially reasonable manner
  • a statement that anyone claiming an interest can bring a court proceeding within 10 days of service if they dispute the lien or the amount

Step 2: How the notice must be delivered

The notice must be personally delivered, sent by registered or certified mail to your last known address, or sent by verified mail and email to your last known address Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. Verified mail is any USPS or private delivery method that gives evidence of mailing, such as first-class mail with a certificate of mailing Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].

Email only counts if your agreement says you consented to late or lien notices by email and you gave your email address in at least two places in it. An emailed notice is treated as delivered once a non-automated reply or a delivery receipt comes back Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. Your last known address is the street, PO box or email address you gave in the agreement or later under it, so keep it current in writing Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].

Step 3: Advertising and the sale

Section 182 says the notice must warn that the goods will be advertised and sold, and that the sale must be commercially reasonable Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. It doesn't spell out how the ads work. New York's general lien-sale article (Lien Law sections 200 to 204) covers liens on personal property in the lienor's possession, other than a few listed types such as warehouse and carrier liens FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[2]. For property worth $100 or more, it requires a public auction in the city or town where the lien arose, advertised once a week for 2 consecutive weeks in a local newspaper, with the sale at least 15 days after the first ad FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[3]. How these general rules fit together with section 182 for a particular sale is a question to put to a lawyer.

A sale isn't unreasonable just because a better price was possible at another time or place, but selling more goods than needed to cover the debt is generally not commercially reasonable Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].

How to stop the sale

Option 1: Pay before the sale

Under the general lien-sale article, you can redeem at any time before the sale by paying the amount due on the lien plus legitimate expenses already spent serving the notice and advertising. You're then entitled to your property FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[4]. Ask for an itemized payoff amount in writing, pay in a way that leaves a record, and get a receipt and written confirmation that the sale is cancelled.

Option 2: Challenge the lien in court within 10 days

If you think the facility isn't entitled to a lien, or the amount is wrong or more than the fair value of its services, you can bring a special proceeding within 10 days of being served the notice, in any court that could hear a claim for that amount. If you win, the court cancels or reduces the lien. If the facility wins in whole or part, the court fixes the amount and the sale may go ahead 5 days after you're served the judgment and notice of entry, unless you redeem first Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. That deadline is short, so contact free legal help right away.

If the facility won't give your things back

Refusing to return your goods after you pay the occupancy fees the law allows is an unlawful detention of goods. You can sue for damages and the return of your goods, and a judgment can be up to three times your actual damages plus reasonable attorney fees. The New York Attorney General can also ask a court to stop violations, and a court can impose a civil penalty of up to $1,000 per violation Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. You can file a complaint with the Attorney General New York State Attorney General[6].

Leftover money from the sale

Under the general lien-sale article, the facility keeps enough to pay its lien and the costs of advertising and sale. It holds any balance for you and must notify you that it's holding it. If nobody claims it within 30 days of the sale, the facility must deposit it with the treasurer or chamberlain of the city or village, the supervisor of the town, or in New York City the Commissioner of Finance, where the sale was held. That office pays it out on demand with satisfactory proof of identity. After 5 years unclaimed, it becomes part of that local government's general funds FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[5]. Ask the facility in writing what the sale brought in and where any balance went.

Other New York rules to know

  • Charges must match your agreement: the agreement must state the actual monthly charge and every other charge in dollars Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. Compare any late or lien bill against it.
  • Liability limits: if your agreement caps what the facility owes for loss or damage, it must say you can ask in writing to raise that cap, show the rates for doing so, and include a request form Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].
  • Rent increases: section 182 has no rent-increase notice period, so your agreement controls Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. See My storage rent went up. What can I do?

Frequently asked questions

How long before a storage unit is auctioned in New York?
Lien Law 182 requires a notice that gives you at least 30 days from mailing to pay before the goods are advertised and sold Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1]. New York's general lien-sale article adds that the sale be advertised once a week for two consecutive weeks and held at least 15 days after the first ad FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[3]. Check your notice for the exact dates.
Can a New York storage facility send the lien notice by email?
Only together with verified mail, and only if your occupancy agreement says you consented to late or lien notices by email and you gave your email address in at least two places in the agreement. An emailed notice counts as delivered when a non-automated reply or a delivery receipt comes back Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].
Can I stop a storage auction in New York?
Yes. You can pay what's owed before the sale; New York's general lien-sale article lets you redeem by paying the lien plus legitimate notice and advertising expenses FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[4]. If you think the bill is wrong, you can start a special proceeding in court within 10 days of being served the notice Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].
What if the facility won't give my things back after I pay?
Lien Law 182 calls that an unlawful detention of goods. You can sue for damages and the return of your goods, and a judgment can be up to three times your actual damages plus reasonable attorney fees. The New York Attorney General can also go to court to stop violations Justia (2025 New York Laws) and FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[1].
Do I get the extra money if my New York storage unit sold for more than I owed?
Under the general lien-sale article, the facility keeps the balance for you and must notify you. If nobody claims it within 30 days of the sale, it's deposited with the local treasurer or chamberlain, the town supervisor, or in New York City the Commissioner of Finance, where you can claim it with proof of identity. After 5 years unclaimed, it goes into that local government's general funds FindLaw (current as of January 1, 2026)[5].
